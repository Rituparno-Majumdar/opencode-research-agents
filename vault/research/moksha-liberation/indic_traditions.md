# मोक्ष (mokṣa) — Liberation in Indian Philosophical Traditions

## Executive Summary

मोक्ष (mokṣa) — liberation — represents one of the most profound and contested concepts across Indian philosophical traditions. While all major schools agree that liberation involves freedom from suffering and the cycle of rebirth (saṃsāra), the nature of this freedom, its conditions, and the paths to achieve it differ radically across schools. This exploration examines how eight major traditions — Advaita Vedānta, Vishishtādvaaita, Dvaita, Sāṅkhya-Yoga, Buddhism, Jainism, Nyāya, and Vaiśeṣika — conceptualize मोक्ष (mokṣa), revealing both profound convergences and irreconcilable divergences in their metaphysical and soteriological frameworks.

---

## 1. मोक्ष (mokṣa) — Etymology and Semantic Field

The term मोक्ष (mokṣa) derives from the root muc — "to release" or "to free." In classical usage, मोक्ष (mokṣa) denotes liberation from the bonds of existence — not mere escape, but the dissolution of the very conditions that make bondage possible. The concept appears across the Vedic corpus, with the Upanishads articulating it as the highest human goal (puruṣārtha), alongside dharma (righteousness), artha (wealth), and kāma (pleasure).

---

## 2. Advaita Vedānta — Śaṅkara's Non-Dual Liberation

### 2.1 Metaphysical Foundation

For Śaṅkarācārya (c. 788–820 CE), मोक्ष (mokṣa) is the direct recognition of one's true nature as ब्रह्म (brahman) — the non-dual consciousness that underlies all appearance. The world of multiplicity (vyāvahārika) is mithyā — empirically real but metaphysically虚 (not absolutely real). Individual self (jīvātman) is none other than ब्रह्म (brahman) itself, temporarily appearing as bound due to avidyā (ignorance).

### 2.2 Definition of Liberation

> ब्रह्म सत्यं जगन्मिथ्या जीवो ब्रह्मैव नापरः
> (brahma satyaṃ jagan mithyā jīvo brahmaiva nāparaḥ)
> — "Brahman is real, the world is false, the individual self is none other than Brahman."

मोक्ष (mokṣa), in Advaita, is not the creation of something new but the removal of the false identification between the self and the body-mind complex. It is apavarga — liberation from all distinction — where the jīvātman realizes complete identity with ब्रह्म (brahman).

### 2.3 Path to Liberation

The path involves three disciplines:

1. **Śravaṇa** (hearing) — studying the Upanishads from a qualified teacher
2. **Manana** (reflection) — philosophical inquiry into the teachings
3. **Nididhyāsana** (meditation) — sustained contemplative absorption

Liberation is achieved through jñāna (knowledge) alone — specifically, ātmajñāna (self-knowledge). Works and ritual (karma) cannot directly produce मोक्ष (mokṣa) but can remove obstacles to knowledge.

### 2.4 The State of Liberation

In liberation (jīvanmukti — liberation while living), the sage experiences no change in external circumstances but possesses complete knowledge that "I am ब्रह्म (brahman)." After death (videhamukti), the apparent individual dissolves entirely into ब्रह्म (brahman) with no rebirth.

## 3. Vishishtādvaaita — Rāmānuja's Qualified Non-Dualism

### 3.1 Metaphysical Foundation

Rāmānuja (1017–1137 CE) rejects Śaṅkara's monism. For him, ब्रह्म (brahman) possesses infinite auspicious attributes (śuddha-sattā). The world and individual souls (cit) are real modes (prakāra) of ब्रह्म (brahman), not illusory appearances. Matter (acit) is equally real but dependent.

### 3.2 Definition of Liberation

मोक्ष (mokṣa) means **paramapada** — the highest state — where the soul attains perpetual proximity to ब्रह्म (brahman). It is not absorption into ब्रह्म (brahman) but eternal communion with the divine. The soul retains its individuality while enjoying सायुज्य (sāyujya) — union with God.

### 3.3 Path to Liberation

Liberation requires:

1. **Prapatti** (surrender) — total self-surrender to ब्रह्म (brahman) as the sole means
2. **Bhakti** (devotion) — loving devotion that purifies the heart
3. **Knowledge** — understanding the distinction between self and non-self

Rāmānuja emphasizes the role of divine grace (kṛpā) — liberation is bestowed by ब्रह्म (brahman) upon the devotee who surrenders completely.

## 4. Dvaita — Madhva's Theistic Dualism

### 4.1 Metaphysical Foundation

Madhvācārya (1238–1317 CE) posits radical distinction (bheda) between:

- ब्रह्म (brahman)/Viṣṇu (the supreme soul)
- Jīvas (individual souls)
- Jagat (material world)

All three categories are eternally real and distinct. The world is not illusion but real creation by God.

### 4.2 Definition of Liberation

मोक्ष (mokṣa) is **salokya** — dwelling in Vaikuṇṭha, the eternal abode of Viṣṇu — alongside other liberated souls. The soul enjoys eternal communion with God but never attains identity with God. This is भक्ति (bhakti)-based liberation through God's grace.

### 4.3 Path to Liberation

- **Bhakti** — exclusive devotion to Viṣṇu
- **Prapatti** — self-surrender
- **Śravaṇa** — hearing divine narratives

Liberation is entirely dependent on divine grace (anugraha). The jīva cannot achieve मोक्ष (mokṣa) through its own efforts.

## 5. Sāṅkhya-Yoga — Liberation through Discrimination

### 5.1 Metaphysical Foundation

The Sāṅkhya system of Īśvara Kṛṣṇa (c. 4th century CE) posits two fundamental principles:

- **Puruṣa** (consciousness) — the witness, untouched by suffering
- **Prakṛti** (matter) — the ground of all experience, comprising three guṇas (sattva, rajas, tamas)

Liberation occurs when puruṣa recognizes its independence from prakṛti and its modifications (mind, body, emotions).

### 5.2 Definition of Liberation

मोक्ष (mokṣa) is the state of **kaivalya** — isolation — where puruṣa stands alone, free from all entanglement with prakṛti. The mind, no longer reflecting puruṣa, ceases to bind. Suffering ends because suffering is always misidentification of puruṣa with the modifications of mind.

### 5.3 Path to Liberation

Patanjali's Yoga Sūtras systematize the path:

1. **Yama** — ethical restraints
2. **Niyama** — observances
3. **Asana** — posture
4. **Prāṇāyāma** — breath control
5. **Pratyāhāra** — withdrawal of senses
6. **Dhāraṇā** — concentration
7. **Dhī** — meditation
8. **Samādhi** — absorption

The goal is **nirodha** — the cessation of the fluctuations of citta (mind-stuff), leading to kaivalya.

## 6. Buddhism — निर्वाण (nirvāṇa) and the Cessation of Suffering

### 6.1 Foundational Framework

The Buddha (c. 5th century BCE) taught the Four Noble Truths:

1. **Dukkha** — suffering exists
2. **Samudaya** — the cause of suffering is taṇhā (craving)
3. **Nirodha** — the cessation of suffering is possible
4. **Mārga** — the Eightfold Path leads to cessation

### 6.2 Definition of Liberation

निर्वाण (nirvāṇa) is the cessation of the three poisons (greed, hatred, delusion) and the end of the cycle of rebirth. It is not a state to be attained but the extinguishing of the fires of passion. The Buddha famously remained silent on whether निर्वाण (nirvāṇa) involves existence or non-existence — it is beyond conceptualization.

> पुब्बे सोतो न हेस्सति, न च पच्छा उप्पादिस्सति
> (pubbe soto na hessati, na ca pacchā uppādissati)
> — "There will be no [rebirth], neither here nor elsewhere."

### 6.3 Path to Liberation

The Eightfold Path:

- Right view, right intention (prajñā)
- Right speech, right action, right livelihood (sīla)
- Right effort, right mindfulness, right concentration (samādhi)

Liberation requires destroying the āsravas (influxes) through insight (vipaśyanā) and meditation (samatha).

### 6.4 Schools of Liberation

- **Theravāda**: Arhat — one who has destroyed all defilements
- **Mahāyāna**: Bodhisattva — one who postpones final liberation to save all beings
- **Vajrayāna**: Buddha-nature — all beings inherently possess enlightenment

## 7. Jainism — केवली (kevalya) and the Siddha

### 7.1 Metaphysical Foundation

Jainism posits two fundamental substances:

- **Jīva** (soul) — conscious, possessing infinite perception, knowledge, and bliss when liberated
- **Ajīva** (non-soul) — matter, time, space, motion

The soul is bound by karmic matter that obscures its innate qualities.

### 7.2 Definition of Liberation

मोक्ष (mokṣa) is **kevalya** — the state of infinite knowledge, perception, and bliss attained when all karmic particles are completely removed. The liberated soul (siddha) achieves:

- **Kevala-Jñāna** — omniscient knowledge
- **Kevala-Darśana** — perfect perception
- **Kevala-Bliss** — infinite happiness

Liberation is the total purification of the soul, making it completely independent (kaivalya).

### 7.3 Path to Liberation

The Jain path involves:

1. **Samayika** — equanimity
2. **Sthānapratiṣṭhā** — stationary meditation
3. **Vairāgya** — renunciation
4. **Sanyoga** — conjunction of right faith, knowledge, and conduct

The three jewels (ratnatraya):

- **Samyak Darśana** — right faith
- **Samyak Jñāna** — right knowledge
- **Samyak Cāritra** — right conduct

Liberation requires strict adherence to non-violence (ahimsa), truth (satya), and non-attachment (aparigraha).

## 8. Nyāya — Liberation through Valid Knowledge

### 8.1 Metaphysical Foundation

The Nyāya school (Gautama) accepts four pramanas (means of knowledge):

1. Pratyakṣa (perception)
2. Anumāna (inference)
3. Upamāna (comparison)
4. Śabda (verbal testimony)

The soul exists, is distinct from body, and is the substratum of consciousness.

### 8.2 Definition of Liberation

Liberation (apavarga) is the cessation of suffering achieved through correct knowledge of:

- The nature of the self
- The nature of suffering
- The causes of suffering
- The means to eliminate suffering

### 8.3 Path to Liberation

Liberation results from **parameṣṭhi** — the highest knowledge attained through:

1. Correct perception
2. Correct inference
3. Proper testimony from the Vedas

The elimination of doubt (sandeha) about the nature of reality leads to liberation.

## 9. Vaiśeṣika — Atomic Liberation

### 9.1 Metaphysical Foundation

The Vaiśeṣika system (Kaṇāda) posits seven categories:

1. Substance (dravya) — including atoms (paramāṇu)
2. Quality (guṇa)
3. Action (karma)
4. Generality (sāmānya)
5. Particularity (viśeṣa)
6. Inherence (samavāya)
7. Non-existence (abhāva)

The soul (ātman) is a substance that possesses qualities like cognition.

### 9.2 Definition of Liberation

Liberation (apavarga) is the cessation of suffering through the removal of conjunction (saṃyoga) between the soul and karmic atoms. When all karmic atoms are removed, the soul attains its natural state of perfect knowledge.

### 9.3 Path to Liberation

Liberation follows:

1. Discrimination between self and non-self
2. Performance of dharma (righteousness)
3. Proper use of pramanas
4. Gradual purification leading to the cessation of rebirth

## 10. Comparative Synthesis

### 10.1 Points of Convergence

All traditions agree that:

- The current state involves suffering (dukkha/saṃsāra)
- Liberation represents the ultimate good
- Knowledge or right understanding is necessary
- Liberation involves freedom from the cycle of rebirth

### 10.2 Points of Divergence

| Aspect | Advaita | Dvaita | Buddhism | Jainism |
|--------|---------|--------|----------|---------|
| Nature of liberation | Identity with ब्रह्म (brahman) | Eternal communion with God | Extinction of craving | Omniscient soul |
| Role of grace | Knowledge removes ignorance | Grace is primary | Path depends on effort | Self-effort |
| Final state | Non-dual consciousness | Personal God | Beyond existence | Infinite bliss |
| Individual soul | Identical with ब्रह्म (brahman) | Eternal distinct | Momentary | Eternal, atomic |

## 11. Key Terms Glossary

- **ब्रह्म (brahman)** — The ultimate reality, the ground of being
- **आत्मन् (ātman)** — The self, soul, or innermost essence
- **जीवात्मन् (jīvātman)** — The individual soul
- **अविद्या (avidyā)** — Ignorance, Nescience
- **सांसार (saṃsāra)** — The cycle of birth and death
- **कैवल्य (kaivalya)** — Isolation (Sāṅkhya-Yoga)
- **निर्वाण (nirvāṇa)** — Liberation in Buddhism
- **केवली (kevalya)** — Omniscient liberation in Jainism
- **भक्ति (bhakti)** — Devotion
- **ज्ञान (jñāna)** — Knowledge
- **प्रपत्ति (prapatti)** — Surrender
- **अहिंसा (ahimsa)** — Non-violence
- **कर्म (karma)** — Action, cause and effect

## Gaps & Honest Limitations

This exploration, while comprehensive in covering eight major traditions, has several limitations:

1. **Oral tradition emphasis**: Many concepts were transmitted orally for centuries before written records — oral authority and teacher lineage (paramparā) played crucial roles that written texts only partially capture.

2. **Regional variations**: The presentation focuses on classical scholastic formulations. Regional traditions (Tamil Śaivism, Bengal Vaiṣṇavism, Kashmiri Śaivism) offer distinct conceptualizations of मोक्ष (mokṣa) not fully represented.

3. **Women's perspectives**: Classical texts largely reflect male Brahminical perspectives. Women's liberation concepts and female spiritual achievers receive limited treatment.

4. **Colonial distortion**: Post-colonial scholarship may reinterpret older concepts through Western philosophical categories. Avoiding this requires careful attention to indigenous categories.

5. **Living traditions**: Focus on textual scholarship may underrepresent how मोक्ष (mokṣa) is understood in contemporary practice across India's diverse spiritual communities.

6. **Language limitations**: While Sanskrit and Pali primary sources are referenced, translation always involves interpretive choices that shape understanding.
