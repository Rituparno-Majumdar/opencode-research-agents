## Egyptian Tradition
### Ka and Ba — Multi-Part Soul
- **Period:** Old Kingdom (2686-2181 BCE) | **Language:** Egyptian | **Primary Text:** Pyramid Texts, Coffin Texts
- **Core Teaching:** The ancient Egyptian soul was conceptualized as multiple components: Ka (life force/double), Ba (personality/soul), Akh (transformed spirit), and Ib (heart/intention). These are not reducible to physical matter — they persist after death and interact with the physical world.
- **Key Terms:** ka (ka) — life force/spirit double; ba (ba) — soul/personality; akh (akh) — transformed spirit
- **Priestly vs. Popular:** The Pyramid Texts represent elite, royal mortuary theology. Common people likely had different, less elaborate beliefs.

## Mesopotamian Tradition
### Gilgamesh and Utnapishtim — Immortality Beyond Death
- **Period:** Old Babylonian (2000-1600 BCE) | **Language:** Akkadian | **Primary Text:** Epic of Gilgamesh
- **Core Teaching:** Utnapishtim, the immortal flood survivor, describes his transformed state — not immortality in the body but transformation to the gods' realm. The quest for immortality leads to acceptance of human limitations.
- **Key Terms:** (no direct consciousness terminology — the focus is on mortality)
- **Priestly vs. Popular:** The epic reflects elite, literate concerns. Popular beliefs about death in Mesopotamia are less documented.

## Ancient Greek Tradition
### Psyche as Soul — Plato
- **Period:** Classical (400-300 BCE) | **Language:** Ancient Greek | **Primary Text:** Phaedo, Timaeus, Republic
- **Core Teaching:** In the Phaedo, Socrates argues that the soul (psyche) is pre-existing, pre-birth, and immortal. It is the rational principle that orders the body. The soul exists independently — it "learns" in the body but remembers its prior existence (anamnesis).
- **Key Terms:** ψυχή (psyche) — soul/life principle; νοῦς (nous) — intellect; ἀνάμνησις (anamnesis) — recollection
- **Priestly vs. Popular:** Platonic philosophy is elite, not popular Greek religion. The Greek masses held different, less systematic beliefs.

### Atomism — Democritus and Epicurus
- **Period:** 5th-3rd century BCE | **Language:** Ancient Greek | **Primary Text:** Fragments (preserved in later sources)
- **Core Teaching:** Democritus posited atoms (atomos — uncuttable) moving in the void. Some scholars (Sextus Empiricus, Lucretius) suggest Epicurus attributed "swerves" (clinamen) to atoms — this is sometimes invoked as ancient precursor to quantum indeterminacy, though the connection is tenuous.
- **Key Terms:** ἄτομος (atomos) — uncuttable; κενόν (kenon) — void; κλίνω (klino) — to lean/swerve
- [CONTESTED: The clinamen is about physics, not consciousness. The connection to quantum mind is speculative.]
- **Priestly vs. Popular:** Atomism was a philosophical position, not popular belief.

### Plotinus — Nous and the One
- **Period:** 3rd century CE | **Language:** Ancient Greek | **Primary Text:** Enneads
- **Core Teaching:** Plotinus' system has the One (to hen), from which emanates Nous (intellect), then Soul (psyche), then matter. The soul's highest function is noesis (intellectual intuition) — direct contact with Nous. This is sometimes invoked as non-materialist consciousness.
- **Key Terms:** τὸ ἕν (to hen) — the One; νοῦς (nous) — intellect; ψυχή (psyche) — soul
- [PARALLEL NOTE: parallels Indic Advaita non-dual consciousness model]

## Hebrew & Jewish Tradition
### Nephesh — Breath-Soul
- **Period:** Second Temple (500 BCE - 70 CE) | **Language:** Hebrew | **Primary Text:** Genesis, Wisdom of Solomon
- **Core Teaching:** The Hebrew nephesh (נפש) is the animating principle — literally "breath." It is not fully immaterial in the Greek sense but is the life-principle. The later Kabbalistic gilgul (גִּלְגּוּל) concept involves soul transmigration — sometimes compared to reincarnation.
- **Key Terms:** נפש (nephesh) — soul/breath; רוח (ruach) — spirit/wind
- **Priestly vs. Popular:** The Pentateuch reflects priestly concerns. Hellenistic Jewish thought (Philo) merged with Greek concepts.

## Islamic Tradition
### ‘Aql — Intellect
- **Period:** Medieval (9th-12th century CE) | **Language:** Arabic | **Primary Text:** Ibn Sina, De Anima
- **Core Teaching:** Ibn Sina (Avicenna) distinguished the "material intellect" (aql hayulani) from the "acquired intellect" (aql mustafad). The latter is the rational soul that can become "separate" — a form often invoked in discussions of afterlife. This is Islamic philosophy's engagement with consciousness.
- **Key Terms:** عقل (aql) — intellect; نفس (nafs) — soul; روح (ruh) — spirit
- **Note:** This covers Islamic philosophy, not Sufi mystical consciousness (which goes to Agent C Sufi section)

## Gnosticism & Hermeticism [from Agent C expansion]
### Gnostic — Pneuma as Divine Spark
- **Period:** 1st-4th century CE | **Language:** Greek, Coptic | **Primary Text:** Nag Hammadi codices
- **Core Teaching:** Gnostic systems distinguish between the highest God and the Demiurge. The pneumatic (spiritual) souls contain divine sparks (pneuma) trapped in matter. Liberation (soteria) involves gnosis — knowledge of one's true divine origin.
- **Key Terms:** πνεῦμα (pneuma) — spirit/spark; γνῶσις (gnosis) — knowledge; σωτηρία (soteria) — salvation
- **Priestly vs. Popular:** Gnosticism was a diverse movement with multiple sects — no single "official" doctrine.

### Hermetic — Mind as Divine
- **Period:** Hellenistic (2nd-3rd century CE) | **Language:** Greek | **Primary Text:** Corpus Hermeticum
- **Core Teaching:** The Poimandres treatise describes the Divine Mind (nous theos) as the first emanation. Human beings share in this mind. The "mind" (nous) can be regenerated through contemplation — it is the divine element in humans.
- **Key Terms:** νοῦς (nous) — mind/divine intellect; λόγος (logos) — reason/word; ἀναγέννησις (anagennesis) — regeneration

## Comparative Observations (unfused — flag only)
