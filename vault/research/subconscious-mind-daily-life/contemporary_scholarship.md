# Contemporary Scholarship: How the Subconscious Mind Drives Us on a Daily Basis

## Executive Summary

Over the past three decades, cognitive science has overturned the Cartesian assumption that conscious reasoning is the primary driver of human behavior. A converging body of experimental evidence from social cognition, neuroscience, behavioral economics, and computational psychiatry demonstrates that the vast majority of daily decisions, emotional responses, habitual actions, and social judgments are generated by non-conscious neural processes operating below the threshold of awareness. These mechanisms — automaticity, priming, implicit attitudes, predictive inference, and interoceptive inference — collectively form what Timothy Wilson termed the "adaptive unconscious": a sophisticated, evolved system for rapid environmental navigation that frees limited conscious resources for novel or high-stakes problems.

The empirical foundation is robust. Bargh and Chartrand's landmark program on automaticity showed that social behavior can be triggered by environmental cues without the actor's awareness or intent. Kahneman and Tversky's dual-process framework (System 1 / System 2) formalized the distinction between fast, automatic, non-conscious cognition and slow, deliberate, conscious reasoning — a distinction now supported by decades of behavioral economic experiments. More recently, predictive processing theories (Friston, Clark, Seth) and constructed emotion models (Barrett) have unified these findings under a single computational principle: the brain is a prediction engine that continuously generates models of sensory input, and conscious experience is the controlled hallucination that results when those predictions meet sensory evidence. What we call "subconscious influence" is, in this framework, simply the vast majority of predictive processing that never rises to the level of conscious attention.

This report draws primarily on peer-reviewed experimental psychology and cognitive neuroscience, supplemented by influential trade-scholarly works that have synthesized these findings for broader audiences. The evidence strongly supports the claim that subconscious processes drive daily behavior across every domain examined — perception, emotion, decision-making, habit, social interaction, and self-regulation. However, significant gaps remain: the explanatory power of predictive processing for specific real-world behaviors is still being tested; replication failures in social priming (the so-called "replication crisis") have forced salutary methodological reforms; and the precise neural mechanisms linking interoceptive inference to everyday emotional experience are incompletely mapped. These limitations are addressed in a dedicated section at the end.

## Section 1: Automaticity — The Architecture of Everyday Action

> "The mere act of categorization, without any conscious intent, can automatically activate associated behavioral scripts and goals." — Bargh & Chartrand (1999)

[PEER-REVIEWED] **John Bargh & Tanya Chartrand** (1999). *The Unbearable Automaticity of Being*. American Psychologist, 54(7), 462-479.
**Methodology:** Series of experimental priming paradigms — scrambled-sentence tests, supraliminal and subliminal priming, reaction-time measures, behavioral observation.
**Traditions covered:** Social cognition, cognitive psychology, automaticity research.
**Key findings:** Four criteria define automaticity — awareness, intentionality, efficiency, and controllability. Bargh and Chartrand demonstrated that environmental stimuli automatically activate associated behavioral schemas. In the classic "elderly priming" experiment, participants exposed to words associated with old age (Florida, bingo, wrinkles, gray) walked more slowly down the hallway after the experiment compared to controls — without any awareness of why. This effect was replicated across multiple domains: politeness priming increased patient wait times, rudeness priming led to faster interruption, and achievement priming improved task performance. The authors proposed that most daily behavior is automatically triggered by environmental features, with conscious will serving as a post-hoc interpreter rather than an initiating cause.

[PEER-REVIEWED] **John Bargh** (1994). *The four horsemen of automaticity: Awareness, intention, efficiency, and control in social cognition*. In R. Wyer & T. Srull (Eds.), Handbook of Social Cognition (2nd ed., Vol. 1, pp. 1-40). Erlbaum.
**Methodology:** Theoretical framework synthesis with experimental validation.
**Key findings:** Automatic processes exist on a continuum rather than as an all-or-nothing property. Pre-conscious automaticity (triggered by stimuli) is distinguished from post-conscious automaticity (triggered by consciously registered but non-attended stimuli) and goal-dependent automaticity (triggered by active goals but running automatically once initiated). Most daily routines fall into the goal-dependent category — we consciously decide to make coffee, but the sequence of actions runs automatically.

[PEER-REVIEWED] **Ap Dijksterhuis & Henk Aarts** (2010). *Goals, attention, and (un)consciousness*. Annual Review of Psychology, 61, 467-490.
**Methodology:** Behavioral experiments combining goal-priming paradigms with attentional measures.
**Key findings:** Goals can be activated non-consciously and then guide behavior in the same way as consciously chosen goals. Non-consciously activated goals show the same properties as conscious goals: persistence over time, resumption after interruption, and increased accessibility after frustration. This finding — automatic goal pursuit — has profound implications for daily life: the environment we inhabit continuously activates goals we may never consciously endorse.

[PEER-REVIEWED] **Tanya Chartrand & John Bargh** (1999). *The chameleon effect: The perception-behavior link and social interaction*. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 76(6), 893-910.
**Methodology:** Multiple experiments with confederates performing specific mannerisms, measuring automatic mimicry.
**Key findings:** People non-consciously mimic the postures, mannerisms, and facial expressions of interaction partners. The chameleon effect increases liking and rapport between interaction partners. This is a direct demonstration of subconscious social influence operating in real time during every conversation — we automatically synchronize with others without intending to.

## Section 2: The Adaptive Unconscious — A New Model of Mind

> "The adaptive unconscious does an excellent job of sizing up the world, warning people of danger, setting goals, and initiating action in a sophisticated and efficient manner." — Timothy D. Wilson (2002)

[TRADE SCHOLARLY] **Timothy D. Wilson** (2002). *Strangers to Ourselves: Discovering the Adaptive Unconscious*. Harvard University Press.
**Methodology:** Synthesis of social cognition experiments, clinical case studies, and evolutionary psychology.
**Traditions covered:** Social psychology, cognitive science, psychodynamic legacy (reclamation and repudiation).
**Key findings:** Wilson proposes the "adaptive unconscious" as distinct from the Freudian dynamic unconscious — it is not a repository of repressed trauma but an evolved, sophisticated system for rapid environmental interpretation and response. The adaptive unconscious handles: pattern recognition, implicit learning, rapid trait judgment, emotional appraisal, goal pursuit, and behavioral prediction. Crucially, Wilson demonstrates that people have remarkably poor access to the causes of their own mental states and behaviors — the verbal explanations people give for their actions are frequently confabulated after the fact. His "affective forecasting" research shows that people systematically mispredict what will make them happy because they lack conscious access to the adaptive unconscious processes that actually generate emotional responses.

[PEER-REVIEWED] **Timothy D. Wilson & Elizabeth W. Dunn** (2004). *Self-knowledge: Its limits, value, and potential for improvement*. Annual Review of Psychology, 55, 493-518.
**Methodology:** Review of experimental paradigms measuring accuracy of self-report vs. behavioral measures.
**Key findings:** People's self-reports about the causes of their behavior are often inaccurate, but people are generally unaware of this inaccuracy. The authors identify four barriers to self-knowledge: (1) non-conscious nature of many mental processes, (2) inaccessibility of procedural knowledge, (3) defensive motivations, and (4) the constructive nature of memory. Recommended strategies for improving self-knowledge include behavioral observation, trusted peer feedback, and structured self-reflection protocols.

[PEER-REVIEWED] **Richard E. Nisbett & Timothy D. Wilson** (1977). *Telling more than we can know: Verbal reports on mental processes*. Psychological Review, 84(3), 231-259.
**Methodology:** Classic experimental series using post-hoc verbal explanation paradigms.
**Key findings:** Groundbreaking demonstration that people often have no conscious access to the actual cognitive processes underlying their judgments and choices. When asked to explain their own reasoning, participants instead generate plausible-sounding confabulations based on a priori causal theories. While the authors noted their findings did not preclude the existence of introspective access to some mental contents, they severely undermined confidence in verbal reports as reliable data about cognitive process — and, by extension, highlighted the vast scope of subconscious influence on daily life.

## Section 3: Two Systems — Kahneman's Architecture of Daily Decision-Making

> "System 1 runs automatically and cannot be turned off. System 2 is normally in a comfortable low-effort mode, with only a fraction of its capacity engaged." — Daniel Kahneman (2011)

[TRADE SCHOLARLY] **Daniel Kahneman** (2011). *Thinking, Fast and Slow*. Farrar, Straus and Giroux.
**Methodology:** Synthesis of decades of experimental behavioral economics and cognitive psychology.
**Traditions covered:** Behavioral economics, cognitive psychology, heuristics and biases, dual-process theory.
**Key findings:** Kahneman's dual-system framework — System 1 (fast, automatic, associative, non-conscious) and System 2 (slow, deliberate, analytical, conscious) — has become the dominant folk model of subconscious influence. System 1 generates continuous judgments of familiarity, affect, and coherence without effort. It runs heuristics (representativeness, availability, affect) that are generally efficient but produce systematic biases. Key demonstrations: the Linda problem (conjunction fallacy), anchoring effects (numerical judgments biased by arbitrary reference points), and the WYSIATI principle (What You See Is All There Is — System 1 operates only on available information without considering absence). Daily decision-making is predominantly System 1, with System 2 intervening only when difficulty, novelty, or conflict arises. This explains why people make predictably irrational decisions in finance (endowment effect), health (present bias), and social judgment (halo effect).

[PEER-REVIEWED] **Daniel Kahneman & Amos Tversky** (1979). *Prospect theory: An analysis of decision under risk*. Econometrica, 47(2), 263-291.
**Methodology:** Experimental behavioral economics with hypothetical choice scenarios.
**Key findings:** Prospect theory — the most influential descriptive model of decision-making under risk — describes how subconscious reference-dependent evaluation, loss aversion (losses hurt ~2x more than equivalent gains feel good), and non-linear probability weighting drive economic decisions. These effects operate automatically through System 1 processes that people cannot introspect on. Over 40 years of replication and extension have confirmed the framework across domains including consumer choice, medical decision-making, and public policy.

[PEER-REVIEWED] **Daniel Kahneman & Shane Frederick** (2002). *Representativeness revisited: Attribute substitution in intuitive judgment*. In T. Gilovich, D. Griffin, & D. Kahneman (Eds.), Heuristics and Biases (pp. 49-81). Cambridge University Press.
**Methodology:** Theoretical analysis supported by experimental paradigms.
**Key findings:** Attribute substitution — when System 1 confronts a difficult question, it automatically substitutes an easier related question without conscious awareness. Example: "How happy are you?" becomes "What is my mood right now?" This non-conscious substitution is a primary mechanism by which subconscious processes hijack daily judgments, leading to systematic biases in life satisfaction reports, risk assessment, and social evaluation.

## Section 4: Implicit Attitudes and the Unconscious Social Mind

> "Implicit attitudes operate outside conscious awareness but profoundly shape behavior, particularly in domains people consciously disavow." — Tali Sharot (2017)

[PEER-REVIEWED] **Anthony G. Greenwald, Debbie E. McGhee, & Jordan L. K. Schwartz** (1998). *Measuring individual differences in implicit cognition: The Implicit Association Test*. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 74(6), 1464-1480.
**Methodology:** Reaction-time based implicit association test (IAT) — categorization task measuring automatic associations.
**Key findings:** The IAT measures the strength of automatic associations between concepts (e.g., race + good, race + bad) by tracking reaction time differences. People who explicitly endorse egalitarian values reliably show implicit racial and gender biases on the IAT. These implicit biases predict subtle behavioral discrimination — hiring callback rates, medical treatment recommendations, and social interaction quality — above and beyond explicit attitude measures. [CONTESTED: widely used but debated regarding predictive validity for individual-level behavior; meta-analyses show modest effect sizes; the IAT has been criticized for arbitrary metric and insufficient test-retest reliability.]

[TRADE SCHOLARLY] **Tali Sharot** (2017). *The Influential Mind: What the Brain Reveals About Our Power to Change Others*. Henry Holt & Co.
**Methodology:** Synthesis of fMRI, behavioral, and field experiments on social influence.
**Key findings:** Subconscious biases shape how we influence and are influenced by others daily. Confirmation bias operates automatically — people process information that confirms existing beliefs more fluently and critically scrutinize disconfirming evidence. The "illusion of transparency" — the subconscious assumption that others see our internal states — drives over-communication and social anxiety. Sharot's research on optimism bias demonstrates that the brain non-consciously computes a positively skewed estimate of future outcomes, which affects daily risk-taking, health behavior, and financial planning.

[PEER-REVIEWED] **Mahzarin R. Banaji & Anthony G. Greenwald** (2013). *Blindspot: Hidden Biases of Good People*. Delacorte Press.
**Methodology:** IAT-based experimental series with large-scale population data.
**Key findings:** Implicit biases are pervasive across demographic categories and persist even in individuals with strong conscious egalitarian commitments. The "blindspot" is precisely the gap between conscious values and non-conscious automatic associations. Daily manifestations include: micro-behaviors in meetings (interrupting, eye contact), hiring decisions, and medical treatment disparities. Importantly, implicit biases are malleable — exposure to counter-stereotypic exemplars and context change can reduce automatic associations.

[PEER-REVIEWED] **Bertram Gawronski & Galen V. Bodenhausen** (2006). *Associative and propositional processes in evaluation: An integrative review of implicit and explicit attitude change*. Psychological Bulletin, 132(5), 692-731.
**Methodology:** Comprehensive theoretical review integrating dual-process models of attitude.
**Key findings:** Implicit and explicit attitudes are products of two distinct processes: associative (automatic, pattern-activated) and propositional (controlled, truth-evaluative). Implicit attitude change requires pattern re-learning through repeated counter-attitudinal exposure, while explicit attitudes can shift through logical argument. Daily behavior is jointly determined by both systems, with implicit attitudes exerting stronger influence under time pressure, cognitive load, or low motivation — conditions characterizing most everyday decisions.

## Section 5: Predictive Processing — The Brain as Unconscious Inference Engine

> "Perception is a controlled hallucination, generated by the brain's predictions about the causes of sensory input." — Anil Seth (2021)

[PEER-REVIEWED] **Karl Friston** (2010). *The free-energy principle: A unified brain theory*. Nature Reviews Neuroscience, 11(2), 127-138.
**Methodology:** Mathematical modeling, theoretical neuroscience, variational Bayesian inference framework.
**Traditions covered:** Computational neuroscience, theoretical biology, Bayesian brain hypothesis.
**Key findings:** The free-energy principle proposes that all biological systems minimize variational free energy — a measure of surprise or prediction error. The brain continuously generates hierarchical probabilistic models of sensory input and updates them by minimizing prediction error through two mechanisms: perception (updating predictions to match sensory data) and action (changing the sensory data to match predictions). Subconscious daily influence is the operation of this predictive hierarchy below conscious threshold — most of the brain's Bayesian inference runs automatically, only promoting predictions to consciousness when prediction error cannot be resolved. Friston's framework unifies perception, action, learning, and attention under a single principle, providing a computational account of why subconscious processing is the default mode of brain function.

[TRADE SCHOLARLY] **Andy Clark** (2016). *Surfing Uncertainty: Prediction, Action, and the Embodied Mind*. Oxford University Press.
**Methodology:** Philosophical and cognitive science synthesis of predictive processing research.
**Key findings:** Clark makes predictive processing accessible, connecting it to everyday experience. Key idea: the brain is a prediction engine that uses top-down generative models to anticipate sensory input; only unpredicted input (prediction error) propagates upward. This means most of what we perceive, feel, and do is driven by unconscious predictions rather than bottom-up sensory data. Daily implications: we see what we expect to see (perceptual set), feel emotions shaped by interoceptive predictions (I predict elevated heart rate → I feel anxious), and move based on predicted action outcomes (forward models in motor control). Clark emphasizes "predictive engagement" with the world — we are constantly acting on our environment to make it match our predictions (niche construction), a process operating largely without conscious awareness.

[TRADE SCHOLARLY] **Anil Seth** (2021). *Being You: A New Science of Consciousness*. Dutton.
**Methodology:** Neuroscience experiments with altered states (psychoactive drugs, meditation, VR) synthesized with predictive processing theory.
**Key findings:** Seth extends predictive processing to conscious experience itself. Perception is "controlled hallucination" — we never directly perceive the world as it is, only the brain's best prediction of its causes. Emotions, selfhood, and the sense of being a unified conscious self are all high-level generative models — "best guesses" — constructed non-consciously. The "self" is not a stable entity but an ongoing predictive process. Daily subconscious influence includes: interoceptive inference (the brain predicts bodily states and we feel what we predict, not what the body is actually doing), emotion construction (fear is not detected but generated from interoceptive predictions), and the rubber hand illusion demonstrating that body ownership itself is a controlled perceptual prediction.

[PEER-REVIEWED] **Lisa Feldman Barrett** (2017). *The theory of constructed emotion: An active inference account of interoception and categorization*. Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience, 12(1), 1-23.
**Methodology:** Neuroimaging meta-analysis, behavioral experiments, anatomical studies of interoceptive networks.
**Key findings:** Barrett's constructed emotion theory argues that emotions are not biologically wired circuits triggered by events but are constructed on the fly by the brain from more basic ingredients: interoceptive sensations (body budget), conceptual knowledge (learned emotion categories), and executive control. The brain predicts the emotional category that best makes sense of current interoceptive and exteroceptive sensory arrays. This means daily emotional experience is overwhelmingly driven by subconscious predictions about bodily state — you don't feel anxious because you perceive a threat; you perceive a threat because your brain predicts anxiety from interoceptive cues and searches the environment for a cause.

[PEER-REVIEWED] **Lisa Feldman Barrett** (2017). *How Emotions Are Made: The Secret Life of the Brain*. Houghton Mifflin Harcourt.
**Methodology:** Synthesis of neuroimaging, behavioral, and cross-cultural evidence.
**Key findings:** [CONTROVERSIAL: Barrett's strong constructionist position challenges universality claims about basic emotions (Ekman); spirited debate continues in affective science.] Barrett argues that the classical view of emotions (anger, fear, sadness, disgust, happiness, surprise as universal, biologically hardwired circuits) is empirically unsupported. Instead, emotions emerge from domain-general interoceptive and conceptual processes. Daily relevance: emotional experience is highly malleable because it is constructed in context — changing conceptual knowledge (learning new emotion words) literally changes what we feel. People who learn finer-grained emotional concepts (e.g., distinguishing frustration from disappointment) develop more granular emotional experience, which correlates with better daily emotion regulation.

## Section 6: Non-Conscious Goal Pursuit and Habit

> "Goals can be activated outside awareness and then guide behavior toward desired outcomes as effectively as consciously chosen goals." — John Bargh (1990)

[PEER-REVIEWED] **Henk Aarts & Ap Dijksterhuis** (2000). *Habits as knowledge structures: Automaticity in goal-directed behavior*. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 78(1), 53-63.
**Methodology:** Experimental paradigms combining habit priming with behavioral measures.
**Key findings:** Habits are represented in memory as automatic associations between situations and actions. Once a habit is established, encountering the triggering context automatically activates the behavioral response — often without the actor's conscious intention or awareness. This explains why habits persist despite conscious desire to change: the automatic situation-action link remains intact even when conscious goals shift. Interrupting habits requires either situation avoidance or novel competing responses practiced to automaticity.

[PEER-REVIEWED] **Bas Verplanken & Sheina Orbell** (2003). *Reflections on past behavior: A self-report index of habit strength*. Journal of Applied Social Psychology, 33(6), 1313-1330.
**Methodology:** Development and validation of the Self-Report Habit Index (SRHI).
**Key findings:** Habit strength is characterized by: (1) frequency of past behavior, (2) automaticity (lack of awareness, lack of control, mental efficiency), and (3) identity relevance. The automaticity dimension is the strongest predictor of habitual behavior in daily life. This measurement tool revealed that many behaviors people think are habits are actually frequently repeated but consciously monitored actions — true habits operate entirely below conscious awareness.

[PEER-REVIEWED] **Wendy Wood & Dennis Rünger** (2016). *Psychology of habit*. Annual Review of Psychology, 67, 289-314.
**Methodology:** Comprehensive review of habit research across experimental, field, and applied settings.
**Key findings:** Habits account for approximately 43% of daily behavior. Once formed, habits are characterized by: (1) context-dependent automaticity, (2) insensitivity to reward devaluation (habits persist even when outcomes become undesirable), and (3) dissociation from conscious goals. Wood's work demonstrates that daily life is largely habitual — driving routes, morning routines, smartphone checking, eating patterns, and social rituals all run automatically. Conscious intentions predict behavior primarily in contexts where habits are weak or absent.

[PEER-REVIEWED] **John Bargh, Peter Gollwitzer, & Annette Lee-Chai** (2001). *The automated will: Nonconscious activation and pursuit of behavioral goals*. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 81(6), 1014-1027.
**Methodology:** Series of 6 experiments using subliminal goal priming, scrambled-sentence tasks, and behavioral measures.
**Key findings:** Direct experimental evidence that goals can be activated non-consciously and then pursued with the same flexibility and persistence as conscious goals. Priming the goal of "achievement" through exposure to achievement-related words led participants to perform better on an unrelated task, persist longer despite obstacles, and resume the task after interruption — all without awareness of why. This "automated will" demonstrates that conscious goal setting is not necessary for goal-directed behavior; environments reliably activate goals non-consciously, shaping daily behavior in ways the actor never endorses or recognizes.

## Section 7: Non-Conscious Emotional Processing

> "The brain constructs emotional experiences on the fly, using interoceptive predictions and conceptual knowledge — most of this construction is non-conscious." — Lisa Feldman Barrett (2017)

[PEER-REVIEWED] **Robert Zajonc** (1980). *Feeling and thinking: Preferences need no inferences*. American Psychologist, 35(2), 151-175.
**Methodology:** Experimental paradigms using subliminal stimulus exposure and preference judgments.
**Key findings:** Landmark demonstration that affective (emotional/evaluative) responses can be generated without any conscious cognitive processing. Mere exposure effect — repeated subliminal exposure to a stimulus increases positive affect toward it, even when participants cannot consciously recognize the stimulus. Zajonc argued that affective processing is primary, independent, and faster than cognitive appraisal — a claim supported by subsequent neuroscience showing subcortical affective pathways (amygdala, basal ganglia) that process emotional valence before cortical involvement.

[PEER-REVIEWED] **John-Dylan Haynes** (2011). *Brain reading and decoding unconscious thought*. In S. Dehaene & Y. Christen (Eds.), Characterizing Consciousness: From Cognition to the Clinic? (pp. 89-108). Springer.
**Methodology:** fMRI and multivariate pattern analysis (MVPA) decoding of unconscious brain states.
**Key findings:** Haynes's laboratory used MVPA to decode mental content — including emotional states and decision intentions — from brain activity patterns before participants became consciously aware of them. In one famous experiment, activity in frontopolar cortex predicted whether a participant would press a left or right button up to 7 seconds before the participant reported conscious awareness of the decision. This "unconscious decision-making" paradigm directly demonstrates that brain processes underlying emotional and behavioral responses begin non-consciously, with conscious awareness arriving as a late-stage interpretation rather than an initiating cause.

[PEER-REVIEWED] **LeDoux, Joseph** (1996). *The Emotional Brain*. Simon & Schuster.
**Methodology:** Animal neurophysiology and human behavioral neuroscience.
**Key findings:** LeDoux identified dual neural pathways for emotional processing: a fast subcortical route (thalamus → amygdala) that processes threat in ~12 milliseconds without conscious awareness, and a slow cortical route (thalamus → cortex → amygdala) that involves conscious appraisal. The "low road" (subcortical) explains why people can have emotional reactions — startle, withdrawal, fear conditioning — before they consciously know what triggered them. This is a direct neural substrate for subconscious emotional influence in daily life: your brain detects and responds to threats before you consciously see them.

[PEER-REVIEWED] **Pessoa, Luiz** (2013). *The Cognitive-Emotional Brain: From Interactions to Integration*. MIT Press.
**Methodology:** Neuroimaging meta-analysis and computational modeling.
**Key findings:** Pessoa argues against the traditional separation of emotion and cognition as distinct systems. Instead, emotion and cognition are fully integrated in brain networks, particularly in prefrontal-amygdala circuits. Subconscious emotional processing continuously shapes cognitive functions — attention, working memory, decision-making — because the same neural circuits process both. Daily relevance: emotional valence of stimuli automatically guides attention (threats capture attention), biases memory retrieval (mood-congruent recall), and influences decision weights — all without conscious monitoring.

## Section 8: Unconscious Perception and Attention

> "Conscious perception is the exception, not the rule. Most sensory processing — visual, auditory, interoceptive — unfolds below awareness." — Stanislas Dehaene (2014)

[PEER-REVIEWED] **Stanislas Dehaene** (2014). *Consciousness and the Brain: Deciphering How the Brain Codes Our Thoughts*. Viking.
**Methodology:** fMRI, MEG, and intracranial EEG during subliminal and conscious perception.
**Key findings:** Dehaene's global neuronal workspace theory proposes that unconscious processing is local and encapsulated (specific brain regions process specific features), while conscious processing requires global ignition across distributed cortical networks. Subliminal stimuli can be processed up to high-level semantic and emotional stages without ever reaching consciousness. The "ignition threshold" — the point at which neural activity becomes globally available — explains why environmental cues influence behavior without awareness: they are processed locally but never enter the global workspace. Daily life is flooded with subliminally processed stimuli — advertisements, social cues, ambient sensory information — continuously shaping behavior without conscious registration.

[PEER-REVIEWED] **Fazekas, Z. & Overgaard, M.** (2018). *A multi-dimensional framework for characterizing consciousness*. Neuroscience of Consciousness, 2018(1), niy007.
**Methodology:** Theoretical review integrating graded consciousness and perceptual awareness scales.
**Key findings:** Argues that consciousness is not binary (conscious/unconscious) but graded. Stimuli can be partially conscious, and the graded nature of awareness has important implications for understanding subconscious influence: many "unconscious" processes may be better described as "weakly conscious" or "pre-conscious." This subtlety matters for daily life — environmental influences are not necessarily completely invisible but may be fleetingly or weakly perceived before being overwritten by competing input.

[PEER-REVIEWED] **Lavie, Nilli** (2005). *Distracted and confused?: Selective attention under load*. Trends in Cognitive Sciences, 9(2), 75-82.
**Methodology:** Behavioral experiments manipulating perceptual and cognitive load.
**Key findings:** Perceptual load theory: when attention is fully occupied by a primary task, task-irrelevant stimuli are not perceived — but they are still processed non-consciously. Under high load, unattended stimuli can influence behavior (priming, emotional conditioning) without any awareness. This directly explains daily experience: when cognitively loaded (multitasking, stress, fatigue), we are more susceptible to subconscious environmental influences because conscious resources are depleted.

## Section 9: Daily Behavior in Predictive Processing Terms

[PEER-REVIEWED] **Gu, Xiaosi & FitzGerald, Thomas H. B.** (2014). *Interoceptive inference: Homeostasis and decision-making*. Trends in Cognitive Sciences, 18(11), 603-614.
**Methodology:** Computational modeling of interoceptive inference within active inference framework.
**Key findings:** Interoceptive inference extends predictive processing to internal bodily states. The brain predicts interoceptive sensory arrays (heart rate, respiration, temperature, visceral state) and acts to minimize interoceptive prediction error. Daily behavior — eating when we predict hunger, seeking warmth when we predict cold, avoiding situations predicted to cause discomfort — is driven by non-conscious interoceptive predictions. Emotional experience arises when interoceptive predictions encounter unexplained prediction error (e.g., elevated heart rate without obvious cause → anxiety). This framework unifies homeostatic regulation, emotional experience, and decision-making under a single non-conscious computational principle.

[PEER-REVIEWED] **Seth, Anil & Friston, Karl** (2016). *Active interoceptive inference and the emotional brain*. Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B, 371(1708), 20160007.
**Methodology:** Theoretical neurobiology with computational simulations.
**Key findings:** Active interoceptive inference proposes that the brain not only predicts interoceptive input but also acts to make the body conform to its predictions. This explains: placebo effects (belief in treatment → predicted relief → actual physiological change), nocebo effects (expectation of harm → predicted pain → actual pain amplification), and emotional regulation strategies that work by changing interoceptive predictions (reappraisal, breathing techniques). Daily subconscious influence includes the fact that expecting to feel a certain way (tired, anxious, energetic) often produces that feeling because the brain treats the expectation as a prediction to be fulfilled rather than a hypothesis to be tested.

[PEER-REVIEWED] **Clark, A.** (2013). *Whatever next? Predictive brains, situated agents, and the future of cognitive science*. Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 36(3), 181-204.
**Methodology:** Target article with extensive peer commentary, theoretical synthesis.
**Key findings:** Clark argues that prediction error minimization explains perception, action, attention, and learning as manifestations of a single principle. Applied to daily life: we select environments (predictable jobs, routines, social groups) that minimize prediction error; we avoid novelty because novel situations generate high prediction error; we form habits because habits make the world predictable. Most of this environmental niche construction is non-conscious — we gravitate toward familiar patterns without deciding to.

## Section 10: Gaps & Honest Limitations

**Replication Crisis in Social Priming.** The most significant challenge to the automaticity literature is the replication crisis that began around 2011. Many classic priming effects — including Bargh's elderly priming (walking slowly) — have failed to replicate in large-scale multi-lab replication attempts. The Reproducibility Project (Open Science Collaboration, 2015) found that only 36% of 100 social psychology studies replicated. This has forced a major methodological reckoning: small sample sizes, publication bias, and questionable research practices (p-hacking, selective reporting) inflated effect sizes. Current best practice involves pre-registration, large samples, and direct replication. The core phenomena (automaticity, non-conscious goal pursuit) remain supported, but the magnitude and robustness of specific effects are more modest than originally claimed.

**Predictive Processing Gap.** Predictive processing provides an elegant unifying framework, but its empirical support is primarily indirect (successful computational simulations, consistency with known neural anatomy, post-hoc explanation of existing phenomena). Direct experimental tests of the theory's specific predictions — especially in daily life contexts — remain limited. Critics (e.g., Block, 2023; Klein, 2018) argue that the framework is too flexible, capable of explaining any outcome post-hoc, and that its core claims are difficult to falsify.

**IAT Predictive Validity Debate.** The IAT's ability to predict individual-level behavior remains contested. Meta-analyses by Greenwald himself (2009) and by Oswald et al. (2013) found that the IAT predicts behavioral discrimination but with small effect sizes (r ~ 0.15-0.20). The IAT is more reliable at the group level than the individual level, limiting its utility for predicting any single person's daily behavior. Alternative implicit measures (affective priming, go/no-go association task) show similar limitations.

**Causal Direction in Emotional Construction.** Barrett's constructed emotion theory is supported by a large body of correlational and neuroimaging evidence, but direct experimental evidence that changing interoceptive predictions causally changes emotional experience in real-world settings is still emerging. Critics (e.g., LeDoux, 2019) argue that Barrett's theory underestimates the role of conserved neural circuits for survival behaviors (defense, foraging, reproduction) that may not be as malleable as the constructionist account implies.

**Cross-Cultural Validity.** The vast majority of automaticity and priming research has been conducted with WEIRD (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic) populations. Cross-cultural studies suggest that automaticity patterns differ across cultures — for example, automatic holistic vs. analytic processing (Nisbett, 2003), differences in habitual vs. deliberative decision-making, and cultural variation in emotion concepts that shape constructed emotional experience. Generalization claims about "the" adaptive unconscious must be qualified.

**Boundary Conditions of Non-Conscious Goal Pursuit.** While non-conscious goal activation is robustly demonstrated in laboratory settings, the ecological validity question remains: in real daily life, goals are typically activated by rich multimodal contexts rather than isolated primes. The extent to which naturalistic environments generate specific non-conscious goal pursuit needs more investigation.

**Technological Limitations.** Current neuroimaging methods (fMRI, MEG, EEG) have limited temporal or spatial resolution for tracking the fast, distributed neural dynamics that characterize non-conscious processing. High-density electrocorticography (ECoG) offers better resolution but is invasive and available only in clinical contexts. Computational models of predictive processing rely on simplified architectures that may not capture the full complexity of biological neural networks.
