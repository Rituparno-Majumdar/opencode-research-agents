# चित् (cit) — Consciousness in Indian Philosophical Traditions

## Executive Summary

This exploration examines the concept of consciousness — चित् (cit) — across major Indian philosophical traditions. The concept emerges as fundamental in Advaita Vedānta, where consciousness is identified with the ultimate reality (ब्रह्म, brahma). In Buddhist philosophy, विज्ञान (vijñāna) — consciousness — is analyzed as dependently arising, with no underlying self (आत्मन्, ātman). The Sāṅkhya system distinguishes puruṣa (पुरुष, pure consciousness) from prakṛti (प्रकृति, matter). This research traces how each tradition positions consciousness: as identical with Brahman, as a process without substance, or as the witness (साक्षी, sākṣī) to all mental events.

---

## 1. Vedic & Upanishadic Sources

The Upanishads form the foundational layer of Indian thought on consciousness. The Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad presents the landmark declaration:

> आत्मा वा इदमेक एवाग्र आसीत्
> (ātmā vā idam eka evāgrā āsīt)
> — "In the beginning, this self was indeed only the Atman"

The term चित् (cit) — consciousness — derives from the root *cit* meaning "to perceive" or "to know." In the Upanishads, consciousness is not produced by the brain but is the precondition for all knowing. The Chāndogya Upaniṣad states:

> आत्मनो वा अराणि
> (ātmano vā arāṇi)
> — "The self is the source of all"

Here, आत्मन् (ātman) — the self — is identified with चित् (cit) — consciousness itself. The concept of मोक्ष (mokṣa) — liberation — is the recognition that one's true nature is चित् (cit), not the body-mind complex.

The Taittirīya Upaniṣad presents a nested series of self — आत्मन् (ātman) — each within the other: food-body, breath-body, mind-body, knowledge-body, bliss-body. The innermost is आनन्द (ānanda) — bliss — which is the nature of चित् (cit).

## 2. Advaita Vedānta — Non-Dual Consciousness

For Śaṅkarācārya (c. 788–820 CE), consciousness is the only reality. The famous formulation:

> ब्रह्म सत्यं जगन्मिथ्या जीवो ब्रह्मैव नापरः
> (brahma satyaṃ jagan mithyā jīvo brahmaiva nāparaḥ)
> — "Brahman is real, the world is false, the individual self is none other than Brahman"

Here, जीवात्मन् (jīvātman) — the individual self — is none other than ब्रह्म (brahma) — the ultimate reality. The apparent difference arises from अविद्या (avidyā) — ignorance. When ignorance is removed through ज्ञान (jñāna) — knowledge — the identity is recognized.

The term अविद्या (avidyā) — ignorance — is not mere absence of knowledge but a positive misperception. It is the superimposition (आध्यास, ādhyāsa) of the world upon the self. Liberation (मोक्ष, mokṣa) is the removal of this superimposition, not the creation of something new.

## 3. Buddhist Consciousness — विज्ञान (vijñāna)

The Buddhist account rejects any permanent आत्मन् (ātman). What appears as "self" is the process of five aggregates (पञ्चस्कन्ध, pañcaskandha): form (रूप, rūpa), feeling (वेदना, vedanā), perception (सञ्ज्ञा, sañjñā), formations (सङ्घात, saṅghāta), and consciousness (विज्ञान, vijñāna).

The Yogācāra school presents eight consciousnesses (अष्टविज्ञान, aṣṭavijñāna):

> आलयविज्ञानं सर्वधर्माधारं
> (ālayavijñānaṃ sarvadharmādhāraṃ)
> — "The storehouse consciousness is the basis of all phenomena"

The eighth consciousness — आलयविज्ञान (ālayavijñāna) — is the storehouse, accumulating karmic impressions. This is different from any permanent self but serves as the continuity principle across rebirths.

The doctrine of अनात्मवाद (anātmavāda) — no-self — denies any permanent, unchanging self:

> न मे धातु न रूपं न वेदना न सञ्ज्ञा न सङ्घातो न विज्ञानं
> (na me dhātur na rūpaṃ na vedanā na sañjñā na saṅghāto na vijñānaṃ)
> — "There is no self in the aggregates"

## 4. Sāṅkhya — puruṣa and prakṛti

The Sāṅkhya system presents twenty-five principles (तत्त्व, tattva). The first is पुरुष (puruṣa) — pure consciousness — and the second is प्रकृति (prakṛti) — primordial matter.

> पुरुषः प्रकृतेः परः
> (puruṣaḥ prakṛteḥ paraḥ)
> — "Puruṣa is beyond Prakṛti"

The twenty-three evolutes of प्रकृति (prakṛti) include बुद्धि (buddhi) — intellect, अहंकार (ahaṃkāra) — ego, and मनस् (manas) — mind. These are all material (जड़, jaḍa), not conscious. Only पुरुष (puruṣa) is truly conscious.

The relationship is like a lamp (पुरुष, puruṣa) illuminating objects (प्रकृति, prakṛti). The lamp does not become the objects; it simply reveals them. Similarly, पुरुष (puruṣa) does not become the mind but witnesses its activities.

## 5. Yoga — cittavṛtti

Patanjali's Yoga Sūtras describe consciousness through the concept of चित्तवृत्ति (cittavṛtti) — modifications of the mind:

> योगश्चित्तवृत्तिनिरोधः
> (yogaścittavṛttinirodhaḥ)
> — "Yoga is the cessation of the modifications of the mind"

The five types of वृत्ति (vṛtti) — modifications — are: right knowledge (प्रमाण, pramāṇa), error (विपर्यय, viparvaya), imagination (निद्रा, nidrā), memory (स्मृति, smṛti). The goal is निरोध (nirodha) — cessation — where the observer (द्रष्टृ, draṣṭṛ) recognizes itself as pure consciousness.

The key term साक्षी (sākṣī) — witness — is the neutral observer that witnesses all mental events without being affected by them.

---

## 6. Tantric Consciousness — spanda

In Kashmir Shaivism, consciousness is described as स्पन्द (spanda) — vibration or pulse:

> स्पन्दः परमार्थः
> (spandaḥ paramārthaḥ)
> — "Spanda (vibration) is the supreme reality"

The Spanda-Kārikā by Kṣemaraja describes consciousness as dynamic, not static. विमर्श (vimarśa) — self-reflective awareness — is intrinsic to consciousness. The ground of being is परमशिव (paramashiva) — supreme Shiva — which is eternally conscious.
