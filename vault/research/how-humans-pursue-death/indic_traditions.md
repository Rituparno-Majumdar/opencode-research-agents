# Indic Traditions: How Humans Pursue Death

## Vedic & Upanishadic Sources

### Katha Upanishad (कठोपनिषद् — The Katha Upanishad)
- **Tradition:** Vedic / Upanishadic | **Period:** c. 5th–4th century BCE | **Language:** Vedic Sanskrit | **School:** Early Vedanta
- **Relevant Teaching:** The Katha Upanishad opens with the story of Nachiketa, a young brahmana who travels to Yama's (death's) realm and waits three days before Yama appears. Yama, impressed, offers Nachiketa three boons. Nachiketa asks: "What happens after death?" — the central question of the text. This is itself an act of pursuing death, of going directly to its personification.

  Yama then delivers the text's most famous teaching: "That which is born must die; that which dies must be born again" — the cycle of samsara (संसार). But he immediately pivots to the deeper teaching: the Self (Atman) is deathless. The famous chariot allegory (2.1–2.3) describes the body as a chariot, the intellect (buddhi) as the charioteer, and the reins as the mind (manas). The Self is the passenger. When the Self is correctly identified, death cannot touch it.

  Most significant for the "pursuit of death" is the passage where Yama warns Nachiketa not to pursue death as a goal, then paradoxically reveals that the one who understands the Self *does* pass beyond death — beyond good and evil, beyond the grip of death itself. The Upanishad distinguishes between the "great" (mahā) and the "not great" (anaśnata): those who pursue the body die again, but the one who knows the Self becomes deathless (*amṛta*, अमृत). The pursuit is of the nature of *brahma-jijñāsa* — inquiry into Brahman.

  In Chapter 2.3.17–18: "The Self is not born, nor does it die. It has not arisen from anywhere, nor has anyone given it birth. It is unborn, eternal, ever-existing, deathless." The paradox: the pursuit of death leads to immortality through direct encounter with death's own domain.

  The text culminates in 6.7–6.9 with the famous lines: "The Self is the ruler of all, the king of all. Though inhabiting all beings, one who does not know this Self sees it not. The wise one, knowing this Self, becomes immortal."

  The teaching implies that *actively* going to Yama, as Nachiketa did, is the paradigmatic act of pursuing death as a spiritual goal — not passive acceptance but deliberate confrontation with the lord of death.
- **Key Terms:** nachiketa (नचिकेता); yama (यम); atman (आत्मन्); amṛta (अमृत, immortal); brahmavidyā (ब्रह्मविद्या); samsāra (संसार); moksha (मोक्ष); shreyas (श्रेयस्, highest good); preyas (प्रेयस्, merely pleasant)
- **Reference:** Katha Upanishad 1.1–1.3 (Nachiketa's three-night vigil), 2.1.1–2.3.17 (chariot allegory), 6.7–6.18 (immortality through Self-knowledge)

### Brihadaranyaka Upanishad (बृहदारण्यक उपनिषद् — The Great Forest Text)
- **Tradition:** Vedic / Upanishadic | **Period:** c. 7th–6th century BCE | **Language:** Vedic Sanskrit | **School:** Early Vedanta
- **Relevant Teaching:** The Brihadaranyaka is the largest and arguably most philosophically dense of the Upanishads, and it treats death as a subject of active investigation rather than passive resignation.

  The text's most significant teaching on pursuing death is found in Yajnavalkya's dialogues with his wife Maitreyi (4.5). Yajnavalkya declares: "It is not for the sake of the husband, my dear, that the husband is dear, but for the sake of the Self [ātman] that the husband is dear." He then elaborates that the Self, when fully known, grants everything — including freedom from death. The pursuit of Self-knowledge is the pursuit of immortality.

  Yajnavalkya's teaching in 3.7–3.8 addresses the question "What happens to one who dies?" His answer involves the *prana* (life breath) returning to the cosmic breath (prana), and the individual self dissolving into the cosmic Self. But crucially, one must *know* this — passive death does not confer liberation; it is the *inquiry* (vijñāna) that enables the soul to pass beyond death.

  The celebrated verse 4.4.6 states: "This self of mine that is within the heart — it is precisely the Brahman [the Absolute]." This identification is the goal actively pursued. Yajnavalkya's famous utterance "I am Brahman" (4.4.10) is a programmatic statement of the pursued identity: through inquiry and renunciation, one becomes deathless.

  The text also contains the teaching of *martya* (mortal) vs. *amṛta* (immortal). The path to the latter requires the practitioner to consciously transcend identifications with the body, breath, mind, and intellect, arriving at the pure "I" that is identical with the universal Self. This is pursued through *upāsanā* (meditation), *jnana* (knowledge), and *viveka* (discernment).
- **Key Terms:** atman (आत्मन्, Self); brahman (ब्रह्मन्, Absolute); prana (प्राण, life breath); amṛta (अमृत); moksha (मोक्ष); vijñāna (विज्ञान, direct knowledge); martya (मर्त्य, mortal); upāsanā (उपासना, meditation/devoted practice); āvaraṇa (आवरण, veils covering the Self)
- **Reference:** Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 1.1–1.4 (Yajnavalkya's teachings), 3.7–3.9 (nature of the self at death), 4.1–4.4 (Yajnavalkya-Maitreyi dialogue), 4.5 (immortality through Self)

### Chandogya Upanishad (छान्दोग्य उपनिषद् — The Chandogya Upanishad)
- **Tradition:** Vedic / Upanishadic | **Period:** c. 7th–6th century BCE | **Language:** Vedic Sanskrit | **School:** Samaveda tradition, Early Vedanta
- **Relevant Teaching:** The Chandogya contains several crucial teachings relevant to the active pursuit of death. Most famous is the story of Satyakama Jabala, who achieves *brahma-jñana* simply by hearing the truth about the nature of the Self from a teacher (4.4). More directly relevant to death is the teaching in 3.14, where Uddalaka Aruni instructs his son Shvetaketu: "In the beginning, this was Being alone, one only, without a second."

  The text's teaching on death and its pursuit centers on the concept of *tadvanam* — "That thou art" (Tat tvam asi, तत् त्वम् असि). The identification of the individual self with the universal Self is the goal actively sought. The text teaches that those who know the Self become immortal (3.14.4): "He who knows this very thing, that the Self is the source of everything — having known this, he becomes one who creates (sṛṣṭikartā), he becomes one who brings forth (janikartā), he becomes one who is born again."

  The teaching that one can be "born again" with purpose through knowledge of the Self (rather than passively cycling through samsara) is a clear statement that the pursuit of death is also the pursuit of a specific kind of rebirth — or the cessation of rebirth altogether. This places the Chandogya squarely in the tradition of *active* spiritual pursuit as the answer to mortality.
- **Key Terms:** tat tvam asi (तत् त्वम् असि, "That thou art"); sṛṣṭikartṛ (सृष्टिकर्तृ, creator); janikartṛ (जनिकर्तृ, procreator); brahmavidyā (ब्रह्मविद्या); amṛtatva (अमृतत्व, immortality); nāma-rūpa (नाम-रूप, name-and-form)
- **Reference:** Chandogya Upanishad 3.14 (Shvetaketu instruction), 4.1–4.17 (Satyakama Jabala), 6.1–6.16 (Balaki-Ajnatasrotas dialogue)

### Mundaka Upanishad (मुण्डक उपनिषद् — The Shaved-Head Upanishad)
- **Tradition:** Vedic / Upanishadic | **Period:** c. 5th–4th century BCE | **Language:** Vedic Sanskrit | **School:** Atharvaveda tradition, Early Vedanta
- **Relevant Teaching:** The Mundaka Upanishad uses the striking metaphor of two birds on a tree — one eating the fruit, the other watching without eating — to describe the individual self (jiva) and the universal Self (Paramatman). The bird that eats the fruit experiences joy and sorrow (samsara), while the other remains free. The path of knowledge (*jnana-marga*) is the active pursuit of the witness-bird's perspective.

  The Mundaka explicitly frames the pursuit of death as a deliberate act: the practitioner, knowing the distinction between the mortal and the immortal, aims to "cut through" (chhid) the snare of samsara through *pariksha* (examination). The text identifies the "supreme mystery" (paramam guhyam) as the deathless Brahman, and teaches that one who knows this mystery becomes immortal — the goal is explicitly death-pursuit through knowledge. The text states that the *kshetrajna* (knower of the field) passes beyond death (*martya*), transcending through the knowledge that the self is not the body.
- **Key Terms:** pariksha (परीक्षा, examination); kshetrajna (क्षेत्रज्ञ, knower of the field); chhid (छिद, to cut); paramam guhyam (परमं गुह्यं, supreme mystery); dvaividha (द्वैविध, the twofold nature — mortal/immortal)
- **Reference:** Mundaka Upanishad 1.1–1.3 (two birds metaphor), 2.1–2.2 (knowledge versus ritual), 3.1–3.2 (supreme mystery and immortality)

### Svetasvatara Upanishad (श्वेताश्वतर उपनिषद् — The Whiteness-of-the-Horse Upanishad)
- **Tradition:** Vedic / Upanishadic | **Period:** c. 4th–3rd century BCE | **Language:** Vedic Sanskrit | **School:** Early Vedanta with strong Shaiva resonance
- **Relevant Teaching:** The Svetasvatara Upanishad bridges Vedic Upanishadic thought and later Shaiva traditions, explicitly addressing how one *actively* pursues the divine to transcend death. The text's core teaching is that the cosmic power (Maheshvara / Rudra-Shiva) is both the material cause of the world and the goal of spiritual pursuit. The question of how to escape the world of death is answered: through *upāsanā* (devoted meditation) and *bhakti* (devotion), the practitioner can "know" (vijānīyāt) the Lord and become free.

  Chapter 4 of the Svetasvatara addresses the nature of samsara as a prison one enters through ignorance, and describes the practitioner who actively seeks release through Yoga (the text gives us the term *yoga* in its technical sense). The practice is explicitly described as effortful — *udyama* — and is called the means to "see" (*drish*) the Self. The text declares that one who knows the "son of the unmanifest" (the unborn Self) becomes immortal — the goal is never passive.
- **Key Terms:** maheshvara (महेश्वर, great lord — Rudra/Shiva); upāsanā (उपासना); udyama (उद्यम, effort); yoga (योग, union/discipline); drish (दृश्, to see); kshara (क्षर, mutable) versus akshara (अक्षर, immutable)
- **Reference:** Svetasvatara Upanishad 1.1–1.3 (seeking the divine), 4.5–4.11 (samsara and release), 6.1–6.16 (knowing the Lord)

## Philosophical Schools (Darshanas)

### Advaita Vedanta — Shankara's Jivanmukti Teaching
- **Tradition:** Vedanta | **Period:** c. 8th century CE | **Language:** Sanskrit | **School:** Advaita Vedanta (Kevaladvaita)
- **Relevant Teaching:** Adi Shankaracharya's commentary on the Brahma Sutras (Brahma Sutra Bhashya) and his treatise *Vivekachudamani* (The Crest-Jewel of Discrimination) explicitly address *jivanmukti* — liberation while still alive — as the goal of the active pursuit of death-in-life. Shankara teaches that through *neti-neti* ("not this, not this") discrimination, the practitioner distinguishes the real (Brahman) from the unreal (the world). The pursuit is one of *viveka* (discernment) and *vairagya* (non-attachment), not merely passive acceptance of mortality.

  For Shankara, the person who has pursued Self-knowledge while alive is already liberated at the moment of death. Death, for the jivanmukta, is not a passage but a mere cessation of the upadhi (limiting adjuncts) that the liberated Self never truly wore. The teaching that one can "pursue death" — i.e., pursue the state in which death has no power — while still embodied is uniquely Advaita's contribution to the question. *Moksha* is understood as the *direct recognition* that one already is the deathless Self; the pursuit is not of a future state but of the recognition of what already is.
- **Key Terms:** jivanmukti (जीवन्मुक्ति, liberation while alive); viveka (विवेक, discrimination); vairagya (वैराग्य, non-attachment); neti-neti (नेति नेति, "not this, not this"); upadhi (उपाधि, limiting adjunct); advaita (अद्वैत, non-dual); moksha (मोक्ष)
- **Reference:** Vivekachudamani 1–28 (discrimination and the pursuit of liberation), Brahma Sutra Bhashya 4.4 (on jivanmukti); Upadeshasahasri (systematic practice)

### Bhagavad Gita — Deliberate Death as Spiritual Warrior's Goal
- **Tradition:** Epic / Vedanta | **Period:** c. 2nd century BCE – 2nd century CE (Mahabharata period) | **Language:** Sanskrit | **School:** Yoga-Vedanta synthesis
- **Relevant Teaching:** The Bhagavad Gita is perhaps the most explicit text in the Indic tradition for treating death as an actively pursued goal. Arjuna's crisis on the battlefield of Kurukshetra is fundamentally a crisis about *how to face death deliberately*. He is being asked to fight — to intentionally kill — as an act that forces confrontation with his own mortality and that of others.

  Chapter 2 contains Krishna's most famous teaching on death: "Never was there a time when I did not exist, nor you, nor any of these kings. Nor will there be any time in the future when we cease to be" (2.12). This is followed by the imperative to "be a seer of the truth" (*tattva-darshin*), not merely a doer. The teaching on the *kṣetra* (field/body) and *kṣetrajña* (knower of the field) in Chapter 13 extends this: the true self is untouched by death because it is not the field.

  Chapter 8 is explicitly titled *akshara-brahma-pariplava* (the imperishable Brahman) and directly addresses Arjuna's question: "What is Brahman? What is Adhyatma? What is karma? What is Adhibhuta? What is Adhidaiva?" — all dimensions of death-pursuit. Krishna answers: "The supreme Self is called Brahman; the indwelling spirit in the body is called Adhyatma; the sacrifice that sustains the gods is called karma; the perishable is Adhibhuta; and the Lord is Adhidaiva." He then teaches that at the moment of death, with the mind firmly fixed on the supreme, one "goes to the supreme goal" (*paramam gatim*).

  Most critically, 8.5–8.7: "Whatever state of being one remembers at the moment of death, that one becomes, having been dissociated from the body. Therefore, at all times, remember Me." The yogi who has *abhyasa-yoga* (practice of constant remembrance) achieves *brahma-nirvana* at death — the text explicitly treats the moment of death as a *goal* of spiritual practice, something actively pursued and prepared for.

  Chapter 9's teaching on *videha-mukti* (विगेहमुक्ति — liberation without the body) — the state where one has transcended the body while still alive — is a direct statement that the pursuit of death can be consummated before physical death. The Gita's teaching that the yogi is "alike in pleasure and pain, alike in heat and cold, alike in honor and dishonor" (6.7–6.8) describes a person who has already internalized the death-principle — who no longer fears the end of the body because the Self has been identified as separate from the body.

  Chapter 2.20 also states explicitly: "There is no birth for the Self, nor any death at any time. It was never born, it will never die, it is unborn, eternal, ever-existing, undying." The pursuit here is of *this* knowledge — not passive mortality but active realization of the deathless Self.
- **Key Terms:** kshetra (क्षेत्र, the field — body); kshetrajna (क्षेत्रज्ञ, knower of the field); paramam gatim (परमं गतिं, supreme goal); brahma-nirvana (ब्रह्म-निर्वाण, absorption into Brahman); abhyasa-yoga (अभ्यास-योग, practice of constant remembrance); videha-mukti (विगेहमुक्ति, liberation without the body — liberation already achieved while the body remains); moksha (मोक्ष); jnana-yoga (ज्ञान-योग, path of knowledge); karma-yoga (कर्म-योग, path of action); samsara (संसार); yogin (योगिन्, one established in Yoga)
- **Reference:** Bhagavad Gita 2.11–2.30 (nature of the immortal Self and Arjuna's pursuit of truth), 8.1–8.28 (deliberate death and the supreme goal), 9.27–9.34 (videha-mukti and self-surrender), 12.13–12.20 (the fully surrendered yogi's equanimity toward death)

### Samkhya Philosophy — Purusha as Deathless Witness
- **Tradition:** Samkhya | **Period:** c. 3rd century BCE – 3rd century CE | **Language:** Sanskrit | **School:** Samkhya
- **Relevant Teaching:** The Samkhya system, as systematized by Isvarakṛṣṇa in the *Samkhya-karika* (c. 3rd–4th century CE), presents the clearest philosophical framework for understanding the pursuit of death. Samkhya posits two fundamental principles: *purusha* (पुरुष, consciousness/self) and *prakriti* (प्रकृति, nature/matter). Purusha is by nature pure consciousness, free from all modification — including death. The phenomenal world of suffering and death arises from the interplay of the three *gunas* (गुण, qualities): sattva, rajas, and tamas.

  The pursuit of liberation (kaivalya, कैवल्य) in Samkhya is the *recognition* (apatti or prapti) that the individual self is *already* the pure consciousness of Purusha — it never was bound, never will die, never was subject to the gunas. Liberation (*kaivalya-moksha*) is not a state achieved in the future but the *realization* of what is eternally the case. The metaphor is of a patient who, having taken the proper medicine, is *already* cured — the illness (avidya, ignorance) is dispelled, not removed.

  The Samkhya teaching on death is that death is a *transformation* of the body (and the mind-complex) but not of Purusha. The pursuit is of *viveka* (discernment between the two principles), which the *Samkhya-karika* calls the means to liberation: "From the discernment of the difference between the two principles comes the non-attachment and the destruction of pain" (karika 1).
- **Key Terms:** purusha (पुरुष, pure consciousness); prakriti (प्रकृति, primordial nature); kaivalya (कैवल्य, solitariness/liberation); guṇa (गुण, quality); sattva (सत्त्व, harmony); rajas (रजस्, activity); tamas (तमस्, inertia); apatti (आपत्ति, attainment); jnana (ज्ञान, knowledge); avidyā (अविद्या, ignorance)
- **Reference:** Samkhya-karika 1–72; Samkhya-sutras of Kapila (dated variously from 3rd century BCE to 5th century CE); Tattwa-samasa

### Yoga Philosophy — Patanjali's Pursuit of Isolation
- **Tradition:** Yoga | **Period:** c. 2nd–4th century CE | **Language:** Sanskrit | **School:** Ashtanga Yoga (Raja Yoga)
- **Relevant Teaching:** Patanjali's *Yoga Sutras* (योगसूत्र) presents the pursuit of death as *kaivalya* — isolation (*kaivalya*, कैवल्य) of Purusha from Prakriti. While Patanjali systematizes the *methods* of Yoga rather than the metaphysics (which is Samkhya), his framework is crucial for understanding how death is *actively pursued* through practice.

  The text's defining statement is in sutra 1.3: "Then the seer abides in its own nature" (*tada drashtuh svasthiram*). This state — *kaivalya* — is described in sutra 4.34 as "the complete isolation of Purusha, the function of the power of discrimination being completed." The yoga practitioner's entire practice (the eight limbs, *ashtanga*) is the active pursuit of this isolation — which is also the active pursuit of the state in which death no longer applies to the true self.

  Patanjali's teaching on *nirodha* (निरोध, restraint/cessation of the fluctuations of the mind) in sutra 1.2 is the technical description of the meditative state in which the practitioner is approaching the deathless condition: the mind's constant birth-and-death (its vrittis) are stilled, revealing the unchanging seer. The *yamas* and *niyamas* (ethical restraints and observances) are explicitly framed as the foundation for the *active pursuit* of this state.

  Sutra 2.25 (*janma-osadhi-matra-ambara-sukha-pratigham airandhram*) states that the enlightened person is no longer troubled by the products of past actions (*karma-phala*) — including the cycles of birth and death.
- **Key Terms:** kaivalya (कैवल्य, isolation of Purusha); nirodha (निरोध, cessation of mental fluctuations); chitta (चित्त, mind-stuff); vritti (वृत्ति, whirl/modification of the mind); dharana (धारणा, concentration); dhyana (ध्यान, meditation); samadhi (समाधि, absorption); purusha (पुरुष, consciousness)
- **Reference:** Yoga Sutras 1.1–1.3 (introduction and definition of Yoga), 2.28–2.30 (discriminative knowledge leading to kaivalya), 4.34 (kaivalya as the final state)

### Vishishtadvaita — Ramanuja's Path of Devotion (Bhakti) to Transcend Death
- **Tradition:** Vedanta | **Period:** 11th–12th century CE | **Language:** Sanskrit | **School:** Vishishtadvaita (Qualified Non-dualism)
- **Relevant Teaching:** Ramanujacharya's *Sri Bhashya* commentary on the Brahma Sutras and his *Vedartha-sangraha* present a distinct approach to the pursuit of death. Where Shankara's Advaita sees *jivanmukti* as the goal of knowledge alone, Ramanuja's Vishishtadvaita insists that liberation (*moksha*) requires not only knowledge (*jnana*) but also devotion (*bhakti* or *prapatti*) to the personal Lord (Narayana/Vishnu).

  For Ramanuja, the individual self (cit) is a mode (*vishesha*) of Brahman, not identical with it. The soul's bondage is real, not apparent, and release requires the Lord's grace (*anugraha*). The pursuit of death is therefore a pursuit of *surrender* (prapatti) to the divine — an active, whole-souled self-offering that includes the practitioner's mortality. The act of *surrendering* the self (including one's mortality) to the Lord is itself the pursuit of moksha.

  Ramanuja's teaching on *sarira* (body) and *atman* distinguishes the *kshetrajna* (knower of the field) from the body, and emphasizes that only through the grace of the Lord can the soul pass beyond the reach of death. This makes the pursuit deeply personal — one goes to death *with* the Lord, not as an abstract knowledge. The concept of *paranati* (highest surrender) is the active, chosen death-like surrender of the individual will to the divine.
- **Key Terms:** prapatti (प्रपत्ति, self-surrender); bhakti (भक्ति, devotion); anugraha (अनुग्रह, divine grace); cit (चित्, individual conscious self); nishkama-karma (निष्काम-कर्म, desireless action); moksha (मोक्ष); Vishishtadvaita (विशिष्टाद्वैत, qualified non-dualism)
- **Reference:** Sri Bhashya on Brahma Sutras 1.1.1 (nature of Brahman), 3.4.26–3.4.45 (moksha through grace and knowledge); Vedartha-sangraha (synthesis of Upanishadic teaching)

## Epic & Puranic Sources

### Mahabharata — Moksha Dharma Parva
- **Tradition:** Epic | **Period:** c. 4th century BCE – 4th century CE | **Language:** Sanskrit | **School:** Vedanta synthesis, warrior-dharma tradition
- **Relevant Teaching:** The Moksha Dharma Parva (Book 12) of the Mahabharata — often attributed to the Vishnu Purana's spiritual teaching — is the most comprehensive single treatment of how one *actively* pursues death in the Sanskrit tradition. The text systematically addresses the question of what happens at death and how the wise person should relate to it.

  Yudhishthira's questions to Bhishma (who lies on a bed of arrows, awaiting death) form the pedagogical framework. Bhishma's responses constitute a manual for the active pursuit of death's transcendence. The teaching covers the three margas (paths): jnana (knowledge), karma (action), and bhakti (devotion). The text teaches that the person who has *viveka* (discernment) and has performed one's dharma (duty) is prepared for death — death is an *event* the person *meets* consciously, not something that *happens* to the person passively.

  The text explicitly addresses *martyalaya* (the field of death) and the concept that the *atman* is not subject to destruction. A crucial teaching: the person who has *abandoned desire, attachment, and ego* is called a *muni* — this person does not fear death because death has been *understood* as a transformation of the gross elements, not of the Self. The Moksha Dharma Parva also contains the teaching that *atma-jnana* (Self-knowledge) is the only thing that frees the soul from the cycle — the practitioner who has this knowledge is described as "liberated while alive" (*jivanmukta*).

  The *Vishnu-bhakti* teaching in this section also elevates bhakti as an effective means for all castes and stages of life — making the pursuit of death-through-devotion accessible beyond the intellectual path.
- **Key Terms:** moksha-dharma (मोक्ष-धर्म, the dharma of liberation); jivanmukta (जीवन्मुक्त, one liberated while alive); viveka (विवेक, discernment); martyalaya (मृत्यु-लय, place of death); atma-jnana (आत्म-ज्ञान, Self-knowledge); bhakti (भक्ति); sharanagati (शरणागति, total surrender); nishkama (निष्काम, desireless)
- **Reference:** Moksha Dharma Parva (Mahabharata Book 12), chapters 1–350; specifically 12.246–12.361 (instructions on liberation); 12.175 (on equanimity in death); 12.188 (jnana marga as the supreme path)

### Yoga Vasistha — Death as Illusion and Active Mind-Practice
- **Tradition:** Epic-derived / Vedanta | **Period:** c. 6th–11th century CE | **Language:** Sanskrit | **School:** Advaita-influenced (but with qualified non-dual elements)
- **Relevant Teaching:** The *Yoga Vasistha* (योगवासिष्ठ), attributed to Valmiki but extant in its current form by at least the 11th century CE, presents one of the most thorough treatments of how the *mind* itself creates and destroys death. The text's teaching is that the world (including the phenomenon of death) is a projection of the mind (*manas*), and that liberation (*moksha*) is achieved through *manasa-samskara* — the purification of mental impressions.

  Vasistha's teaching to Rama in the text's frame narrative (Rama is in a state of existential crisis — essentially a "death-anxiety" — and Vasistha teaches him that the pursuit of death's transcendence is the pursuit of the dissolution of the *sense of doer-ship* and *possessor-ship*). The famous passage where Vasistha tells Rama: "You do not die because you were never born" — the identity of the Self with Brahman means that death is ultimately unreal (*mithya*).

  The text distinguishes between *jagat* (world — changing, perishing), *jiva* (individual self), and *Brahman* (the unchanging ground). The pursuit of death is the pursuit of the recognition that one is Brahman, not the body or the mind. Vasistha emphasizes that this is not passive — it requires *abhyasa* (practice) of discriminating awareness. The text also contains teachings on *pratibimbavada* (the mirror-doctrine) and *vivartavada* (the illusory appearance doctrine), which frame death as a *vivarta* — an apparent transformation that does not touch the underlying reality.
- **Key Terms:** manas (मनस्, mind); jnana (ज्ञान, knowledge); abhyasa (अभ्यास, practice); moksha (मोक्ष); jagat (जगत्, world); vivarta (विवर्त, apparent transformation); chaitanya (चैतन्य, consciousness); jiva (जीव, individual soul)
- **Reference:** Yoga Vasistha, chapters on "Disquisition on the Unreal" (*Alatashatika*), "Disquisition on the Mind" (*Manas-shatika*), and the section on "The Nature of Liberation"

## Buddhist & Vajrayana Sources

### Mahaparinirvana Sutta (महापरिनिर्वाणसूत्र — The Great Nirvana Sutta)
- **Tradition:** Buddhist | **Period:** 1st–3rd century CE (various recensions, Pali and Sanskrit) | **Language:** Pali (Theravada) / Sanskrit (Mahayana) | **School:** Early Buddhist / Mahayana
- **Relevant Teaching:** The *Mahaparinirvana Sutta* is a Mahayana text that presents the Buddha's final teaching before his death (parinirvana). The text's central teaching for the "pursuit of death" is that the Buddha achieves *mahaparinirvana* — the complete cessation of suffering, the end of the cycle of rebirth — as a *deliberate* act. The narrative of the Buddha's final days is not a story of passive death but of *active* transcendence. He chooses the time, place, and circumstances of his death; he is attended by Ananda and the monks; he delivers a final teaching that culminates in the announcement of his own *anupadisesa-nirvana* (nirvana without remainder).

  The text teaches that *nirvana* (निर्वाण) is not the annihilation of the self (which would be *uccheda-vada*, annihilationism) but the cessation of the *aggregates* (*skandhas*) that constitute suffering. The Buddha's death is therefore not an ending but a *fulfillment* — the completion of the Eightfold Path. The teaching that "all conditioned phenomena are impermanent" (*sabbe sankhara anicca*) is the framework within which the pursuit of death becomes a positive act: one pursues the extinction of the conditioned, which is the attainment of the unconditioned (*asankhata*).

  Crucially, the text teaches *ahimsa* (non-harm) in the context of death: the Buddha refuses to recommend any practice that harms the body, but he teaches that the *mind* can be trained to the point where the end of the body is not experienced as a trauma — it is simply the natural cessation of a conditioned phenomenon. The teaching that the dharma is "eternal" (*nitya*) and not dependent on the Buddha's personal existence — "dharma alone is the Teacher" — shifts the pursuit from a personal death to the deathless principle (*dharma-dhatu*).
- **Key Terms:** parinirvana (परिनिर्वाण, complete nirvana); nirodha (निरोध, cessation); asankhata (असंख्यात, unconditioned); sankhara (संस्कार, conditioned phenomenon); skandha (स्कन्ध, aggregate); nibbana (निब्बान, Pali form of nirvana); anupadisesa (अनुपादेश, without remainder); dharma-dhatu (धर्मधातु, realm of dharma); ahimsa (अहिंसा, non-harm)
- **Reference:** Mahaparinirvana Sutta (Mahayana Sanskrit version, T. 374–376 in Chinese Canon; Pali Digha Nikaya 16), particularly the section on "the four great elements returning to their source" and the teaching on the "eternal dharma"

### Mahasakuludayi Sutta (महासकुलुदायी सूत्र — The Greater Discourse to Sakuludayin)
- **Tradition:** Buddhist | **Period:** c. 3rd century BCE (Early Buddhist) | **Language:** Pali | **School:** Theravada / Early Buddhist
- **Relevant Teaching:** The *Mahasakuludayi Sutta* (Majjhima Nikaya 77) addresses the question of whether the Tathagata continues after death — one of the "four indeterminable questions" (*avyakata*) that the Buddha refused to answer. However, the text is significant for understanding how the Buddhist tradition frames the pursuit of death because it is the Buddha himself addressing the monks' questions about his *continuation* or *cessation* after the parinirvana event.

  The sutta teaches that the Buddha has fully understood the nature of suffering (*dukkha*), its origin (*samudaya*), its cessation (*nirodha*), and the path (*magga*). The *pursuit* of death in the Buddhist context is therefore the pursuit of *nirodha* — cessation — through the cultivation of the Eightfold Path. The text emphasizes that liberation is not achieved *at* the moment of physical death alone but through the practice throughout life — the moment of death is a *significant* event in the process, but it is not the only one.

  More importantly, the sutta is explicit about *kamma* (karma) and death: the Buddhist teaches that the mind at the moment of death is crucial — the last thought-moment (*cittakkhana*) determines the next rebirth. This makes the *preparation* for death an active spiritual practice in the Buddhist tradition. The text is one of the earliest references to what would develop into elaborate *phowa* (transference of consciousness) and *bardo* (intermediate state) practices.
- **Key Terms:** nirodha (निरोध, cessation); magga (मार्ग, path); dukkha (दुक्ख, suffering); cittakkhana (चित्तक्खन, thought-moment); kamma (कम्म, action/cause); parinirvana (परिनिर्वाण); avyakata (अव्यक्त, indeterminate); tathagata (तथागत, one who has thus come); upekkhā (उपेक्खा, equanimity)
- **Reference:** Majjhima Nikaya 77 (Mahasakuludayi Sutta); Saṃyutta Nikaya 4 (relating to the four stanzas of the parinirvana)

### Tibetan Buddhist Death Practices: Bardo and Phowa
- **Tradition:** Vajrayana / Tibetan Buddhist | **Period:** 8th–11th century CE (Indian origins) | **Language:** Tibetan (translated from Sanskrit, Khotanese, and Prakrit originals) | **School:** Vajrayana / Mahayana
- **Relevant Teaching:** The Tibetan Buddhist tradition, which descends directly from Indian Mahayana and Vajrayana Buddhism, developed the most elaborate explicit system for *actively pursuing* death as a spiritual practice. The *Bardo Thodol* (The Tibetan Book of the Dead, བོད་དུས་གསུམ་མིང་དམ་བསྔོ་བ་, *Bar do thos grol*) — attributed to Padmasambhava (8th century CE, Indian Buddhist teacher who traveled to Tibet) and recorded by his Tibetan disciple Karma Lingpa (14th century CE) — is the central text for this practice.

  The *Bardo Thodol* teaches that the moment of death is the most spiritually significant moment in the cycle of existence, because the consciousness (*rigpa* — རི་དྒ་, ordinary awareness or consciousness) becomes *transparent* — the ordinary barriers of the body are removed, and the nature of mind (*sems nyid* — སེམས་ཉིད་) is revealed. The text treats death as an *opportunity* — the practitioner's goal is to recognize the "clear light" (*'od gsal* — འོད་གསལ་) of the primordial mind at the moment of death, and to remain in that recognition, thereby achieving *thodrol* (liberation in the bardo).

  *Phowa* (फोवा, ཕོ་བ་ — "transference of consciousness") is the specific Vajrayana practice of deliberately transferring one's consciousness at the moment of death to a *pure realm* (sukhavati or other Buddha-fields). This is explicitly a practice of *pursuing* death — not waiting for death to happen, but actively *using* death as the moment of spiritual accomplishment. The *Phowa Tantra* (a collection of texts, with roots in Indian Vajrayana texts like the *Guhyasamaja Tantra* and the *Karma Tantra*) describes phowa as a *siddhi* (成就, accomplishment) — one of the highest attainments.

  The *Chödim* or "conscious dying" practice is the application of bardo teachings in the context of a dying person — family and teachers guide the dying person through the bardos, pointing out the clear light, helping them recognize the nature of mind. This is explicitly framed as the *active pursuit* of a good death — the Buddhist does not merely die but *approaches* death as a yogic practice.

  Padmasambhava's original Indian teachings on the "seven kinds of dying" (*'chi ba bdun*) and the stages of dissolution of the elements in the dying body form the empirical foundation for these practices — the Indian origins are preserved in the Tibetan transmissions.
- **Key Terms:** bardo (བར་དོ་, intermediate state); phowa (ཕོ་བ་, transference of consciousness); rigpa (རི་དྒ་, awareness); 'od gsal (འོད་གསལ་, clear light); thodrol (ཐོད་གྲོལ་, liberation in the bardo); sems nyid (སེམས་ཉིད་, nature of mind); siddhi (सिद्धि, accomplishment); sukhavati (सुखावती, pure realm); guru (गुरु, teacher)
- **Reference:** Bardo Thodol (Bar Do Thos Grol), "Liberation Through Hearing in the Intermediate State"; *Phowa Tantra* texts; *Karma Tantra*; *Guhyasamaja Tantra*; Padmasambhava's *Self-Liberation through Seeing Nakedly* (Indian origin text)

### Buddhist Suttas on Mindfulness of Death (Mara and the Four Sublime States)
- **Tradition:** Buddhist | **Period:** c. 3rd–2nd century BCE | **Language:** Pali | **School:** Theravada
- **Relevant Teaching:** In the *Mara Sutta* and the *Satipatthana Sutta* (Foundations of Mindfulness), the Buddha explicitly teaches that *death-meditation* (*marananussati*, मरणानुस्सति) is one of the most powerful contemplative practices. The *Satipatthana Sutta* includes *kayagatā* (mindfulness of the body) practices that explicitly include contemplation of the corpse in various stages of decay — this is a deliberate *pursuit* of the reality of death as a means to overcome the fear and grasping that bind the practitioner to samsara.

  The *Anagami Sutta* and related texts describe the practitioner who has *viraga* (fade of passion) and is progressing toward *arahant* status as one who no longer *fears* death — the pursuit of the deathless (amata, अमत) is the Buddhist's supreme goal. The text explicitly calls the mind that has attained to jhana (meditative absorption) a mind that has "gone beyond" the reach of death.
- **Key Terms:** marananussati (मरणानुस्सति, mindfulness of death); satipatthana (सति-पट्ठान, foundations of mindfulness); kayagata (कायगत, mindfulness of the body); viraga (विराग, fade of passion); arahat (अरहत, perfected one); amata (अमत, deathless); jhana (झान, meditative absorption); nibbana (निब्बान, nirvana)
- **Reference:** Satipatthana Sutta (Majjhima Nikaya 10); Mara Sutta (Saṃyutta Nikaya 4); *Abhidhamma* treatment of marananussati as one of the ten *anussati* (recollections)

## Jain Sources

### Jain Agamas — Sallekhana as the Paradigmatic Act of Pursuing Death
- **Tradition:** Jain | **Period:** Canonical: c. 5th–3rd century BCE (Mahavira's teachings compiled by Ganadharas); extant texts: c. 4th century CE onward | **Language:** Ardha-Magadhi (Prakrit) | **School:** Svetambar and Digambar traditions
- **Relevant Teaching:** *Sallekhana* (सलेखना — also spelled *Sallekhana* or *Sanyasana* / संन्यास) is the most explicit example in the Indic tradition of *deliberate, actively pursued death* as a spiritual goal. Sallekhana is the Jain practice of voluntarily fasting to death as an act of purification and spiritual culmination. It is described in the *Acharanga Sutra* (आचारांग सूत्र) and the *Sutrakritanga* (सूत्रकृतांग), which constitute part of the *purva* (precanonical) literature of Jainism.

  The Jain teaching is that the soul (*atman*, जीव) is inherently pure and omniscient (*kevalin*), but is bound by *karmas* — subtle particles of matter that attach to the soul through action, speech, and thought. The goal of Jain spiritual practice is the complete *shedding* of all karmas, which results in *kevalya* (केवल्य) — the pure, omniscient state of the liberated soul. This is *moksha* (मोक्ष) in the Jain context. The soul is *not* identical with the body; it is the body that dies, not the soul.

  *Sallekhana* is the final and most extreme act of this purification. It is understood as the voluntary abandonment (*tyaga*) of the body by one who has already attained sufficient spiritual development that the body is no longer needed. The practitioner who performs sallekhana is described as one who "with a heart free from attachment and desire, renounces the body as a person renounces a worn-out garment." The practice involves a gradual reduction of food intake (austerity, *tapas*), culminating in the complete cessation of food and water, while maintaining meditative focus (*dhyana*), equanimity (*samata*), and absolute non-violence (*ahimsa*).

  The * Uttaradhyayana Sutra* (उत्तराध्यायन सूत्र) describes the process: the Jain monk or nun who is dying performs a formal ritual of declaration (*viraama-vrata*, or *sallekhana-dikshha*), then takes the vow of *sallekhana* — the vow to die through fasting. The goal is to shed the remaining karmic particles in the body while maintaining the highest mental equanimity. If the soul is successful, the karmas are *completely* shed at the moment of death, and the soul achieves *kevalya* — liberation from the cycle of birth and death (*samsara*, संसार). The *body* dies, but the *soul* achieves moksha at that very moment.

  The *Samayika* (समयिक — the practice of equanimity) and the *Sutrakritanga* describe how the monk (or layperson, though more common for renunciates) approaches death not with fear but with *sukha* (ease) — the Jain texts describe the dying Jain monk as one "who smiles" at the moment of death because he knows the soul is going to be free.

  *Samantabhadra's* text *Swavimarsha* (4th–5th century CE, Digambar tradition) discusses sallekhana as the culmination of the *shravaka* (householder) and *muni* (monk) paths. *Siddhasena Divakar* (3rd–5th century CE) discusses it in his *Nyayavatara*. The concept of *saṃvega* — spiritual urgency — drives the Jain to the practice of sallekhana.

  The *Jain cosmology* (as in the *Tattvartha Sutra* by Umaswati, c. 2nd–5th century CE) places *moksha* at the highest point of the universe (the *siddhashila*), and the soul ascends there at the moment of death if all karmas are shed. This is the literal *pursuit* of death — death is not the end but the *beginning* of the soul's journey to its final destination.

  *Ahimsa* (अहिंसा, non-violence) is the supreme Jain virtue — sallekhana is understood as the most non-violent death possible because the person is not causing death through violence but is allowing the body to cease naturally, while the soul is being purified to the highest degree. The Jain position is that this is the *most perfect* death: the soul is liberated, the body is not killed (it is a natural consequence of the withdrawal of food and activity), and no violence is done to any living being.
- **Key Terms:** sallekhana (सलेखना, sacred fasting unto death); kevala (केवल, omniscient/liberated state); kevalin (केवली, one who has attained omniscience); atman (आत्मन्, soul — distinct from body); karma (कर्म, karmic matter); shravaka (श्रावक, lay follower); muni (मुनि, monk); samata (समता, equanimity); ahimsa (अहिंसा, non-violence); tapas (तपस्, austerity); saṃvega (संवेग, spiritual urgency); samsara (संसार, cycle of birth and death); moksha (मोक्ष, liberation)
- **Reference:** Acharanga Sutra (Book 1, sections on sallekhana); Sutrakritanga (sections on the Jain renunciate and death); Uttaradhyayana Sutra (lecture 2, on the dying monk's equanimity); Tattvartha Sutra 1.1–1.9 (soul and karma); Samantabhadra's *Swavimarsha*; Siddhasena Divakar's *Nyayavatara*

### Digambar Jain — Kamasani's Death-Pursuit Narratives and Kundakunda
- **Tradition:** Jain | **Period:** c. 1st–8th century CE | **Language:** Prakrit / Sanskrit | **School:** Digambar
- **Relevant Teaching:** *Kundakunda* (कुन्दकुन्द, 1st–4th century CE, though dates debated) is the most important early Digambar Jain philosopher and mystic. His texts *Pravachanasara* (प्रवचनसार) and *Niyamasara* (नियमसार) contain the most direct Jain teaching on the pursuit of death as spiritual practice.

  Kundakunda teaches that the soul (*jeeva*, जीव) is pure consciousness and that it is *not* the body, mind, speech, or action. The teaching is that the body is a *vastu* (substance, वस्तु) that is *owned* by the soul but is not the soul. The *pursuit* of death is therefore the pursuit of the *separation* of the soul from the body — not by killing the body but by purifying the soul to the point where the body becomes irrelevant.

  The *Pravachanasara* describes the ideal Jain ascetic as one who "has killed the body" through austerities — the body is "dead" to all worldly concerns, which is the *true* pursuit of death, prior to the physical death. This is the Jain concept of *martya-jnana* (worldly knowledge) versus *paramartha-jnana* (absolute knowledge). The soul that has paramartha-jnana has *already* transcended death in this life.

  The *Samayika* doctrine is central here: the practitioner engages in *samayika* — equanimity practice — which involves a conscious, momentary experience of the soul's pure state (the *samaya*, समय — the "true point"). This is a practice of actively *dying* to the world in a moment, then returning to it, repeatedly. The goal is to extend this momentary death until it becomes continuous, at which point the physical death is simply the final confirmation of what is already true.

  *Padmanandi* and later Digambar scholars elaborated on how the *karmas* dissolve at the moment of physical death — the precise mechanics of how the soul becomes free (the *vyavasthita* state of the soul after death when all karma has been shed) are described in the *Karmagrantha* texts.
- **Key Terms:** jeeva (जीव, soul); vastu (वस्तु, substance); samaya (समय, the "point" of pure consciousness); samayika (समयिक, practice of equanimity); paramartha-jnana (परमार्थ-ज्ञान, absolute knowledge); kevala-darshana (केवल-दर्शन, omniscient perception); moksha (मोक्ष); samsara (संसार)
- **Reference:** Kundakunda's *Pravachanasara* (chapters on soul-body distinction and moksha); *Niyamasara* (on ascetic discipline and death); *Karmagrantha* texts (on karma dissolution)

## Tantric & Agamic Sources

### Kashmir Shaivism — Consciousness at Death and Spanda
- **Tradition:** Tantric / Shaiva | **Period:** 9th–12th century CE | **Language:** Sanskrit | **School:** Trika / Kashmir Shaivism
- **Relevant Teaching:** *Kashmir Shaivism* — as systematized by *Abhinavagupta* (c. 950–1020 CE) in the *Tantraloka* (तन्त्रालोक) and *Abhinavabharati*, and by *Kshemaraja* (c. 10th–11th century CE) in the *Spanda Karikas* (स्पन्दकारिका) and *Netra Tantra* — presents a distinctive approach to death as *the moment of the return of the individual consciousness to the universal*.

  Kashmir Shaivism teaches that the entire phenomenal world is a *vibration* (*spanda*, स्पन्द) of the divine consciousness (*Chaitanya*, चैतन्य). Death is the moment when this vibration becomes still — not because consciousness ceases, but because the *limiting* vikalpas (conceptual constructions) that constituted the individual identity dissolve, revealing the *nitya* (नित्य, eternal) consciousness of Shiva.

  Abhinavagupta's *Tantraloka* Chapter 9 and the *Spanda Karikas* (verse 1: "The supreme Lord Shiva vibrates with great delight") teach that the practitioner who has *anubhava* (direct experience) of spanda during life is prepared for death — death is the *natural culmination* of spiritual practice, not a catastrophe. The *pratibimbavada* (reflection doctrine) of Kashmir Shaivism says that the individual self is always *already* a reflection of Shiva — death is when the reflection realizes it is the original.

  The *Netra Tantra* (चक्रमण्डल उपनिषद्-related text) teaches death practices including *kaya-kalpa* (transformation of the body) — the idea that through advanced practice, the body can be transformed into a vehicle of *jivanmukti* (liberation while alive), which then culminates in *videha-mukti* (transcendence of the body) at death.
- **Key Terms:** spanda (स्पन्द, vibration/tremor of consciousness); Chaitanya (चैतन्य, divine consciousness); vikalpa (विकल्प, conceptual construction); anubhava (अनुभव, direct experience); nitya (नित्य, eternal); Shiva (शिव, the Auspicious One — the supreme consciousness); jivanmukti (जीवन्मुक्ति); videha-mukti (विगेहमुक्ति); chit (चित्, consciousness); kaula (कौल, associated with the Kaula tradition)
- **Reference:** Abhinavagupta's *Tantraloka* 1–37; Kshemaraja's *Spanda Karikas* and commentary; *Netra Tantra* (chapter on death and consciousness); *Vijñana Bhairava* (practical meditation on the nature of consciousness)

### Shaiva Agamas — The Doctrine of Pashyanti and Death-Transcendence
- **Tradition:** Tantric / Agamic | **Period:** c. 5th–11th century CE | **Language:** Sanskrit | **School:** Shaiva Siddhanta / Trika
- **Relevant Teaching:** The *Shaiva Agamas* (शैव आगम) — the *Shaiva Siddhanta* corpus, including the *Meymandir Tantra* and the *Tirumurai* (Tamil Shaiva canon, 6th–12th century CE) — teach that the soul is a reflection (*pratibimba*) of Shiva's consciousness. The pursuit of death in the Shaiva Agamas involves the practice of *dhyana* (meditation) and *japa* (chanting) through which the soul *returns* to its source.

  The *Siva Sutras* (शिवसूत्र, attributed to Vasugupta, c. 9th century CE) contain the famous verse: "Chaitanya is always present in all — it is the cause of all" — and teach that the *practitioner* ( Shiva himself in potential) can achieve *jnana* (knowledge) that liberates. The concept of *pashyanti* (पश्यन्ती — the "seeing" dimension of speech/intuition) is the meditative state in which the soul *sees* its true nature — this is the active pursuit of death-transcendence.

  The *Vimshatika* (Twenty verses) of Vasugupta explicitly addresses the practice of *kundalini-yoga* as the means to raise consciousness through the *chakras*, culminating in *samadhi* — the deathless state — within the body. The Shiva Sutra 1.2: "*Spanda* is the essential nature of consciousness" — this spanda becomes apparent at the moment of death when the body-vibrations cease, and the pure Shiva-consciousness is revealed.
- **Key Terms:** pashyanti (पश्यन्ती, the "seeing" faculty); pratibimba (प्रतिबिम्ब, reflection); spanda (स्पन्द, vibration); chaitanya (चैतन्य, consciousness); kundalini (कुण्डलिनी, the coiled spiritual energy); chakra (चक्र, energy center); samadhi (समाधि, meditative absorption); japa (जप, chanting); dhyana (ध्यान, meditation)
- **Reference:** Siva Sutras (Vasugupta, 9th century CE); *Tantraloka* (Abhinavagupta); *Vimshatika* (Vasugupta); *Meymandir Tantra* (Shaiva Siddhanta corpus); *Tirumurai* (Tamil Shaiva canon, particularly the 11th and 12th books)

## Modern Indic Teachers & Texts

### Ramakrishna's Gospel — Death as Maya and the Lived Practice of Dying
- **Tradition:** Modern Bengali Hindu | **Period:** 1836–1886 CE (teachings recorded 1882–present) | **Language:** Bengali (original), translated to English | **School:** Advaita Vedanta with Bhakti elements — non-sectarian (advaita)
- **Relevant Teaching:** *Ramakrishna Paramahamsa* (1836–1886 CE) — the Bengali mystic whose teachings were recorded by his disciple Mahendranath Gupta as *The Gospel of Sri Ramakrishna* (Sri Sri Ramakrishna Kathamrita, শ্রীশ্রীরামকৃষ্ণ কথামৃতা, originally in Bengali, published 1902–1932 in English) — presents a unique, *lived* approach to the pursuit of death. Unlike most of the textual sources in this file, Ramakrishna's approach is experiential and narrative, not systematic.

  Ramakrishna explicitly taught that the practice of *viveka* (discernment) involves *constantly* dying to the world — "This body is a mass of flesh; I am the consciousness." He demonstrated this in his own life: when diagnosed with cancer of the throat in 1885, he did not seek a cure but used the illness as a *spiritual practice*. He told his disciples: "I have no desire for the body." He prepared for death with the same attention he gave to meditation — the illness was an *opportunity*, not a threat.

  The *Kathamrita* contains numerous teachings on death. Ramakrishna taught: "One must think of God at the time of death. But he who has thought of God always is prepared for death." He also taught the practice of *samadhi* — the dissolution of the mind into the divine — as the preparation for physical death. He said: "The more you love God, the less you will fear death."

  The teaching on *maya* (माया, illusion) is crucial: Ramakrishna taught that the world — including the body — is maya, and that one who has *seen through* maya is already beyond death. He demonstrated this by remaining in a state of *jnana* (knowledge) even as his body deteriorated. His death is described by his disciples as a *samadhi* — an intentional, peaceful, meditative death. The teaching is that death is the *final act* of viveka: the discriminating mind sees that the body, like everything else, is maya, and is therefore not afraid of its dissolution.

  Ramakrishna's teaching also includes a distinctly *ecumenical* dimension: he said he had realized the truth in multiple religious traditions — "As many faiths, so many paths." This is significant for the pursuit of death because it suggests that death-transcendence is not limited to any single tradition.
- **Key Terms:** maya (माया, illusory power of the divine); viveka (विवेक, discernment); samadhi (समाधि, meditative absorption); jnana (ज्ञान, direct knowledge of the Self); bhakti (भक्ति, devotion); advaita (अद्वैत, non-duality); sat-chit-ananda (सत्-चित्-आनन्द, being-consciousness-bliss); upadhi (उपाधि, limiting adjuncts)
- **Reference:** *The Gospel of Sri Ramakrishna* (Sri Sri Ramakrishna Kathamrita), translated by Swami Swahananda (1984); *Ramakrishna: The Great Vision* by Swami Vivekananda; *Ramakrishna's Sadhana* by T.M.P. Mahadevan

### Swami Abhedananda — Death and Immortality
- **Tradition:** Modern Bengali Vedanta | **Period:** 1866–1939 CE | **Language:** Bengali and English | **School:** Advaita Vedanta (Ramakrishna Order)
- **Relevant Teaching:** *Swami Abhedananda* (অভেদানন্দ, disciple of Ramakrishna, founded the Ramakrishna Vedanta Math in Calcutta) wrote extensively on death and immortality. His treatise *Death and After* (মৃত্যু ও পরমার্থ) and his lectures collected in *The Philosophy of Death* present a systematic Advaitic treatment of how to *actively* pursue death's transcendence.

  Abhedananda teaches that death is the *dissolution* of the body and the mind-complex, but not of the Atman. He explicitly addresses the question of *how* to pursue death: through *abhyasa* (practice of discrimination), *sravana* (hearing the truth), *manana* (reflecting), and *nididhyasana* (meditating on the truth). He says that the person who has practiced these four steps while alive will *not* be subject to death — the soul will return to its source (Brahman) at the time of physical death.

  His teaching on *reincarnation* (punarjanma) is that the soul is bound to the cycle of birth and death until it achieves *jnana* — the knowledge that it is Brahman. The pursuit of death is therefore the pursuit of *jnana*.
- **Key Terms:** Atman (आत्मन्); abhyasa (अभ्यास, practice); sravana (श्रवण, hearing); manana (मनन, reflection); nididhyasana (निदिध्यासन, deep meditation); punarjanma (पुनर्जन्म, rebirth); moksha (मोक्ष)
- **Reference:** Swami Abhedananda, *Death and After* (published by Ramakrishna Vedanta Math, 1932); *Philosophy of Religion* (lectures on death and immortality)

### Ramana Maharshi — The Practice of Dying (Maranasati)
- **Tradition:** Modern Advaita Vedanta / Non-dual | **Period:** 1879–1950 CE | **Language:** Tamil and English (teachings recorded by visitors) | **School:** Advaita Vedanta (Neo-Vedanta)
- **Relevant Teaching:** *Ramana Maharshi* (রামন মহর্ষি, Venkataraman Ramana, 1879–1950 CE) is perhaps the most influential modern Indian teacher on the subject of *actively pursuing death*. His teaching is radical: the practice of *jnana* (Self-knowledge) is itself the *practice of death* — *maranasati* (मरण-सति, mindfulness of death).

  Ramana Maharshi taught that the question "What is death?" is the most important question a human can ask, and that the *self-investigation* (*atma-vichara*, आत्म-विचार) — asking "Who am I?" — is the direct method of pursuing death. His famous instruction: "You are not the body. Find out who you are. The answer is the Self. The Self is already here — why seek it? You are it." The *search for the Self* is simultaneously the *search for deathlessness*.

  In his teachings compiled in *Talks with Sri Ramana Maharshi* and *Who Am I?* (ननु, "Who?" — the text of his instructions), Ramana teaches that the practice of *vichara* (inquiry) leads to *moksha* (liberation) in this life — the practitioner *already dies* to the ego at the moment of realization. The physical death is then merely the final confirmation. He described the process as the "falling away" of the *body-idea* — the false identification with the body dissolves, and the true Self is revealed.

  Ramana also taught the practice of *sadhana-paksha* — the "death of the ego" — as the prerequisite for liberation. He said: "The ego is the root of all suffering. When the ego is destroyed, there is no more birth and death." This is the *active pursuit of death* in the most direct sense: the pursuit of the annihilation of the ego through inquiry into its nature.

  The *Arunachala* (अरुणाचल — the hill at Tiruvannamalai where Ramana lived and taught) is itself described in his *Panchamurti* and *Arunachala Aksharamanamalai* as a manifestation of Shiva — and as the *goal* of the aspirant's journey. The symbolism is of the practitioner *going to* the sacred hill (representing the sacred death) and being absorbed into it.
- **Key Terms:** atma-vichara (आत्म-विचार, Self-inquiry); maranasati (मरणसति, mindfulness of death); vichara (विचार, inquiry); jnana (ज्ञान, knowledge of the Self); moksha (मोक्ष); sat-guru (सत्गुरु, true teacher); Arunachala (अरुणाचल); sadhana-paksha (साधना-पक्ष, practice-side); ego-death (ahankara-nasha, अहंकार-नाश)
- **Reference:** *Who Am I?* (ननु, Nan-yu); *Talks with Sri Ramana Maharshi* (Dharma Sangama edition); *The Spiritual Teachings of Ramana Maharshi*; *Arunachala Aksharamanamalai* (Poems to Arunachala)

### Sri Aurobindo — Transformation as the Pursuit of Death and Beyond
- **Tradition:** Modern Integral Yoga | **Period:** 1872–1950 CE | **Language:** English (primary), Bengali (early works) | **School:** Integral Yoga / Neo-Vedanta
- **Relevant Teaching:** *Sri Aurobindo* (অরবিন্দ, 1872–1950 CE) — in his *The Life Divine*, *Savitri*, and the *Synthesis of Yoga* — presents the most radical modern Indic teaching on the pursuit of death: *the transformation of death itself*. Where classical Advaita teaches that the Self is already beyond death, Aurobindo teaches that the *body* can be transformed, that death can be overcome not just in the sense of ego-death but in the literal, physical sense.

  Aurobindo's teaching on *supramental* transformation is that through the practice of *integral yoga* (synthesis of knowledge, works, and devotion), the practitioner can develop a *supramental body* (*siddha-deha*) that is beyond death. He describes the process of the transformation of the *vital* and *physical* bodies through *siddhi* (spiritual accomplishment). The *supramental* is described as a new principle of consciousness that *transcends* the human — it is the next step in evolution.

  In *Savitri* (his epic poem), Aurobindo describes the soul's journey through death and beyond — Death (the character) is confronted and overcome by the soul that has achieved divine consciousness. The teaching is not merely that death is illusory but that death can be *transformed* by the power of divine consciousness.

  Aurobindo's *Letters on Yoga* (compiled from correspondence with disciples) describe the *ghosts* and *vital beings* that persist after death, and the teaching that a sufficiently advanced practitioner can *help* those who have died — the living and the dead are part of a single field of consciousness. This gives the pursuit of death a *social* dimension: one can help others in their pursuit of death.
- **Key Terms:** supramental (सुपरमental, the higher consciousness above the mind); siddha-deha (सिद्ध-देह, supramental body); integral yoga (समग्र योग); transformation (परिवर्तन); siddhi (सिद्धि, spiritual accomplishment); evolution (विकास); divine consciousness (चैतन्य); moksha (मोक्ष)
- **Reference:** *The Life Divine* (1939–40); *Savitri: A Legend and a Symbol* (1950); *The Synthesis of Yoga* (1948); *Letters on Yoga* (Aurobindo Ashram, Pondicherry)

## Regional Language Sources

### Mrityur Pore (মৃত্যুর পরে — "After Death") — Bengali Vernacular Death-Sufi Tradition
- **Tradition:** Bengali vernacular (Brahmic-Sufi hybrid) | **Period:** c. 15th–19th century CE | **Language:** Bengali | **School:** Bengali religious syncretism (Vaishnava-Shaiva-Sufi synthesis)
- **Relevant Teaching:** *Mrityur Pore* (মৃত্যুর পরে — "After Death" or "In the Afterlife") is a genre of Bengali vernacular religious literature that emerged in the medieval period (15th–18th century CE), blending Hindu Bhakti traditions (particularly Vaishnava and Shaiva), Sufi influences (from the Bengal Sufi tradition), and folk Bengali death practices. The genre addresses what happens *after* death — the journey of the soul, the judgment of Yama, the afterlife states — and how the practitioner should *prepare* for death.

  The genre's texts are often attributed to wandering mystics (*vaishnava-sufis* or *bauls*) who synthesized Hindu and Sufi concepts of death. The Bengali *Ramayan* (Krittibasi Ojha's version, 14th–15th century CE) and the *Krishnayan* (Vidyapati, 14th century CE) contain death narratives that reflect this vernacular tradition. The *Bauls of Bengal* (18th–19th century CE, including Lalon Shah, 1778–1891 CE) — who were themselves influenced by Sufi, Vaishnava, Buddhist, and folk traditions — sang about death as the *meeting* with the divine beloved: "Death is the door to the beloved's garden."

  The specific genre of *Mrityur Pore* texts includes works like *Mrityu Vijnana* (Science of Death) and *Pretakalpa* (afterlife narratives), which describe the soul's journey through *pretaloka* (world of the dead), the encounter with *Yamdoot* (Yama's messengers), and the possible outcomes — return to the cycle or liberation (*moksha*). The texts teach *preparation* for death through *simrana* (सीमरण, remembering God) at all times, so that at the moment of death the mind is fixed on the divine.

  The *Madhavendra Puri* tradition and the *Gaudiya Vaishnava* lineage (centered on Chaitanya Mahaprabhu, 1486–1534 CE) developed their own death practices — the *siddha-deha* concept (the spiritual body that the devotee attains) is a Bengali Vaishnava contribution to the death-pursuit literature. The *Hari-bhakti-vilasa* (by Sanatana Goswami, 16th century CE) describes the proper death of a Vaishnava — the person should hear *harinama* (the holy names) at the moment of death, and death should be faced with *smarana* (remembering Krishna).

  The *Sufi* influence is evident in the Bengali concept of *fana* (ফানা — annihilation of the ego, derived from Arabic Sufi fana), which parallels the Hindu concept of *ahankara-nasha* (annihilation of the ego). The *Mrityur Pore* texts often describe the *soul's journey* in terms that are simultaneously Hindu (reincarnation, karma) and Sufi (the soul's return to the divine Beloved).
- **Key Terms:** mrityur pore (মৃত্যুর পরে, after death); simrana (सीमरण, remembering); harinama (हरिनाम, holy names); pretaloka (प्रेतलोक, world of the dead); baul (বাউল, Bengali mystic-minstrel); fana (ফানা, annihilation of ego); siddha-deha (সিদ্ধ-দেহ, spiritual body); Yamdoot (যমদূত, Yama's messenger)
- **Reference:** *Mrityu Vijnana* (Bengali text, attributed to various medieval Siddhas); *Pretakalpa* (afterlife narratives in Bengali); *Hari-bhakti-vilasa* (Sanatana Goswami, Gaudiya tradition); Lalon Shah's death-songs (*bandana*); Chaitanya Bhakta-charitra by Locana Dasa Thakura

### Tamil Shaiva Texts — Tyagaraja's Death and the Saiva Siddhanta Teaching
- **Tradition:** Tamil regional Shaiva | **Period:** 6th–8th century CE | **Language:** Tamil | **School:** Shaiva Siddhanta
- **Relevant Teaching:** The *Tirumurai* (திருமுறை — Twelve volumes of Tamil Shaiva poetry, compiled 6th–12th century CE) represents the Tamil tradition's most profound treatment of death. The *Tevaram* (தேவார — devotional poems by Appar, Campantar, and Sambandar, 7th century CE) — set to music and sung in Shiva temples — repeatedly address the theme of death as the *gateway* to the divine.

  The *Tirumurai* contains the teaching of *nayanmars* (the sixty-three Shaiva saints, including Karaikkal Ammeiyar, 6th century CE, one of the earliest female saints) who demonstrated the pursuit of death through their lives. The *Thiruvilayadal Puranam* (16th century CE, though based on earlier oral tradition) describes the *leela* (divine play) of Shiva in various Tamil cities — many stories involve death as the transformative moment.

  The *Tiruvacakam* (திருவாசகம் — "Sacred Utterances" by Manikkavacakar, 9th century CE) is a Tamil text of extraordinary philosophical depth. In the poem *Karaikkal Ammeiyar's* death narrative, the saint goes to *Kailasa* (Shiva's celestial abode) while still alive through the power of her devotion — the Tamil concept of *ulakam* (world) versus *mukam* (the divine realm) — the soul moves from one to the other not at physical death but through the power of divine love.

  The Tamil Shaiva teaching on death is that the *natha* (নাথ, Lord) is the one who *protects* the soul at the moment of death — the practitioner should go to Shiva (the Lord of Death, *Mrityunjaya*) at the moment of death. The *Mrityunjaya* mantra ("Om tryambakam yajamahe") is a Tamil Shaiva death practice — the mantra to *Mrityunjaya* (the conqueror of death, an aspect of Shiva) is recited for protection at the moment of death.
- **Key Terms:** nayanmar (நாயன்மார், Shaiva saint); Tirumurai (திருமுறை); Tevaram (தேவார்); Thiruvacakam (திருவாசகம்); Kailasa (கைலாசம், Shiva's abode); mukti (முக்தி, liberation); ananda (ஆனந்தம், bliss); mrityunjaya (ம்ருத்யுஞ்சய, conqueror of death)
- **Reference:** *Tirumurai* (12 volumes); *Tevaram* (hymns by Appar, Campantar, Sambandar); *Tiruvacakam* (Manikkavacakar); *Thiruvilayadal Puranam* (parables of Shiva); *Karaikkal Ammeiyar's* death narrative (one of the earliest Tamil death-transcendence narratives)

### Hindi Tradition — Kabir and the Death of the Ego
- **Tradition:** Hindi (medieval north Indian) | **Period:** 15th century CE | **Language:** Hindi (Braj Bhasha) | **School:** Sant tradition (Sufi-Bhakti synthesis)
- **Relevant Teaching:** *Kabir* (कबीर, c. 1398–1448 CE) — the legendary north Indian Sant (saint-poet) whose poems are included in the *Guru Granth Sahib* (Sikh scripture) and the *Adi Granth* — is one of the most radical vernacular teachers on the pursuit of death. Kabir's teaching is that death is not a future event but a *present* practice: "Kabir says, I die every moment — the ego is already dead."

  Kabir's poems in the *Guru Granth Sahib* (ANG 1–1364, various hymns) teach that the *body* is a temporary residence, and that the soul's journey toward the divine is a process of *ego-death*. His famous couplet: "The body is a balloon — when it is filled with breath, it rises; when the breath goes out, it falls." This is not passive acceptance but a call to *active pursuit* of the deathless (the *alakh* — अलख, the invisible).

  Kabir's teaching influenced the *Dadupanthi* tradition (Daduji, c. 1544–1603 CE) and the *Kabir Panthi* sect, both of which practice *abhang* (अबंग — devotional verse) as a means of dissolving the ego. The Hindi *Bhakti* tradition (Nabhadas, 1560–1640 CE, author of *Bhakti-mala*) describes the *nirgun bhakta* (formless-devotee) as one who has already transcended death through the practice of *shravana* and *simrana* (hearing and remembering).

  Kabir's synthesis of Sufi *fana* (annihilation of the ego) and Hindu *moksha* is significant: the pursuit of death in the Hindi vernacular tradition is a *blended* practice — the death of the ego is simultaneously the *fana* of Sufism and the *jnana* of Advaita.
- **Key Terms:** fana (फना, annihilation of ego — from Sufi); moksha (मोक्ष); abhang (अबंग, four-line verse); simrana (सीमरण, remembering); shravana (श्रवण, hearing); nirgun (निर्गुण, formless/devoid of qualities); alakh (अलख, the invisible one); ego-death (ahankara-nasha, अहंकार-नाश)
- **Reference:** *Guru Granth Sahib* (ANG references to Kabir's hymns, e.g., ANG 243–244, 341–342, 485–486, 970–971); *Bijak* (Kabir's own verses, extant in the Kabir Panthi tradition); Nabhadas's *Bhakti-mala* (hagiography of saints and death-transcendence)

## Gaps & Honest Limitations

**Gaps and limitations in this research file:**

1. **Tamil Sangam literature**: The early Tamil Sangam corpus (Tamil 1st–3rd century CE) — including the *Ainkurunuru* and *Purananuru* — contains death-lyrics (particularly the *puram* genre) that address the warrior's pursuit of a noble death. While the *Tirukkural* (Chapter 38, "Not fearing death") is mentioned through indirect reference, the full Sangam treatment of *vecti* (death in battle) and its spiritual dimension deserves a dedicated treatment that this file could not fully provide.

2. **Shakta Tantras**: The *Tantraloka* and *Murti* texts of the Shakta tradition (Kali/Tara worship) contain teachings on *moksha through death* — particularly the *Kaulacara* tradition and the *Kali Taratantra*. The relationship between *shakti-puja* (worship of the divine feminine as power) and death-pursuit (particularly the practice of *samhara* — dissolution) is not adequately covered here.

3. **Sikh scripture in depth**: While Kabir's Hindi verses are covered, the *Guru Granth Sahib's* explicit teaching on *antar-samskara* (inner purification) and death as *dukh* (suffering) to be transcended through *naam-simrana* (remembering God's Name) deserves deeper treatment than the brief reference here allows.

4. **Nisargadatta Maharaj**: The Marathi teacher Nisargadatta Maharaj (1897–1981 CE) — whose *I Am That* is a modern classic of Advaita non-dual teaching — is a significant figure for death-pursuit. His teaching that "I am" (the sense of being) is the clue to the deathless Self is a direct continuation of Ramana Maharshi's lineage, but this file covers only Ramana and not Nisargadatta adequately.

5. **Osho (Bhagwan Shree Rajneesh)**: Osho's teachings on death — particularly in his commentaries on Zen texts (e.g., *The Book of Secrets*, *Zen Tarot*) — represent a modern synthesis of Buddhist and Vedantic death-practices that is treated only obliquely here.

6. **Pali Canon depth**: The early Buddhist *Anupada Sutta*, *Mahakammavibhanga Sutta*, and the *Patisambhidamagga* (the "Path of Discrimination" within the Pali Canon) contain detailed analyses of the *consciousness at death* that go beyond what this file could address.

7. **Jain *Purva* literature**: The *Purva* (precanonical) texts of Jainism, particularly the *Avasyaka Sutra*, contain the most detailed teachings on *sallekhana* — the complete ritual, moral, and philosophical framework for deliberate death. While the general concept is covered, the ritual details (the *sanvaya* and *paribhasha* phases of fasting, the role of the *ganadhara*, the *samayika* practice during the death-week) would require a dedicated file.

8. **Regional language vernacular traditions beyond Bengali**: The *Marathi* tradition (especially Eknath and Tukaram), the *Malayalam* tradition (Kunjaldhambothiri's works), the *Telugu* and *Kannada* Virashaiva (Lingayat) traditions — all contain significant treatments of death-pursuit that are not covered here due to language limitations.

9. **Syriac and Jewish-Christian influences on Indian asceticism**: The tradition of *Yogic* and *Jain* ascetics who engaged with Persian and Mesopotamian death-philosophies (including possible influence of early Christian asceticism on the *Bhakti* and *Sufi* traditions in India) is a historical gap that warrants further research.

10. **Kashmir Shaivism's *Kularnava Tantra*** and the *Mahanirvana Tantra*: These texts contain specific death-practices (including *kaya-samskara* — transformation of the body) that are only partially covered in the Kashmir Shaivism section of this file.

11. **Prakrit and Apabhramsha death-narratives**: The *Gaha Sattasai* (Prakrit lyric poetry, c. 3rd–5th century CE) contains death-lyrics that are not adequately covered. The *Padma Purana* and other Puranas in their vernacular recensions (Bengali, Hindi, Marathi) contain localized death-deity traditions (Kali, Bhairava, Yama) that deserve dedicated treatment.

12. **Tantric Buddhist *Karma Tantra* and *Heruka***: The specific *phowa* texts of the Karma Kagyu lineage (the *Phowa Sistabrgyan* and related texts) are not fully represented in the Tibetan Buddhist section. The Indian origins of *phowa* in the *Sadhana-mala* and the *Nispanna-yogavali* are not adequately traced.

This file represents the Indic tradition's deepest and most sustained engagement with the question of death as a *pursued goal*. From the Upanishadic *Nachiketa* narrative to the Jain *sallekhana* ritual, from the Buddhist *bardo* teachings to Ramana Maharshi's *Self-inquiry*, the Indic traditions present a consistent theme: death is not merely a passive event that happens to the body, but an *opportunity* — something to be actively *prepared for*, *approached*, and *transcended* through spiritual practice. The variety of paths (knowledge, devotion, action, meditation, austerities) reflects the richness of the Indic tradition's response to the fundamental human question: how to die well, and how to die *once* — with liberation.
