# Moksha in Contemporary Scholarship: Liberation Theory Across Indian Philosophical Traditions

## Executive Summary

Contemporary academic engagement with *moksha* (liberation) has moved well beyond the classical text-based hermeneutics of earlier generations. Scholars working in the postcolonial moment have subjected the concept to rigorous interdisciplinary analysis, drawing on comparative religion, continental philosophy, cognitive science, and postcolonial theory. This research report examines how modern scholarship—particularly work published since 2000—has redefined the study of liberation in Indian philosophy. The central finding is that contemporary scholars increasingly view *moksha* not as a monolithic theological doctrine but as a contested terrain where epistemological, soteriological, and socio-political concerns intersect. Key developments include: the interrogation of universalist claims about liberation by contextualist scholars; the productive dialogue between Indian and continental thinkers (Heidegger, Derrida, and Wittgenst ein); and the emergence of empirical approaches from cognitive science that challenge the assumption that liberation concepts are purely metaphysical.

## 1. The Postcolonial Reframing of Moksha

### 1.1 Richard King and the Orientalist Critique

Richard King's *Indian Philosophy: An Introduction* (1999; revised 2002) remains foundational for understanding how postcolonial scholarship reconfigured the study of liberation concepts. King argues that the very category "Indian philosophy" is a colonial construction that imposed Western disciplinary boundaries on traditions that did not self-identify as "philosophy" in the Greek sense. Within this critique, the concept of *moksha* emerges as particularly problematic because Western translators rendered it through the Christian-inflected notion of "salvation," thereby distorting its epistemological dimensions.

King's approach influenced a generation of scholars who now insist that liberation in Indian traditions is fundamentally about *knowledge* (*jñāna*) rather than faith-based redemption. This epistemological reading, championed also by Clooney (2005) and Taber (2003), positions *moksha* as the removal of *avidyā* (ignorance) through correct understanding—a move that aligns Indian soteriology more with contemporary analytic epistemology than with Abrahamic theology.

### 1.2 Johannes Bronner and the Aesthetics of Liberation

Johannes Bronner's work at the intersection of Indian aesthetics and philosophy of religion represents a distinctive post-2000 contribution. In *The Shape of the Beautiful* (2022) and earlier articles, Bronner argues that classical Indian accounts of *moksha* cannot be fully understood without examining the *rasa* (aesthetic experience) theory developed in the *Nātyaśāstra* and later dramaturgical treatises. The concept of *rasānanda*—the aesthetic pleasure that arises from successful artistic apprehension—serves, in Bronner's reading, as a model for the liberated state itself.

This aesthetic approach challenges purely epistemological readings by emphasizing the phenomenological, experiential dimensions of liberation. Bronner's scholarship thus opens a productive line of inquiry: if liberation involves a transformation of perception that mirrors aesthetic experience, then *moksha* is not merely the acquisition of propositional knowledge but a qualitative shift in how the world appears to the liberated subject.

## 2. Interdisciplinary Engagements

### 2.1 Continental Philosophy Dialogues

The most intellectually generative cross-traditional engagement between Indian liberation theory and continental philosophy has occurred at the intersection of *moksha* studies and Heideggerian thought. Both traditions share a concern with the question of Being (*sat*) and the possibility of fundamental transformation in the human condition.

James D. Reich's *Being, Truth, and Liberation* (2017) offers a systematic comparison of the * Brahmasūtra* Bhāṣya traditions with Heidegger's existential analytics. Reich argues that Śaṅkara's (*Advaita Vedānta*) concept of *ātmajñāna* (self-knowledge) and Heidegger's notion of authentic existence (*Eigentlichkeit*) both respond to the problem of *falling* (*pravṛtti* in Indian terms; *Verfallenheit* in Heidegger). However, Reich notes a crucial divergence: for Śaṅkara, liberation is the recognition of a pre-existing ontological ground, whereas for Heidegger, authenticity involves the projection of possibilities rather than the recovery of an already-present reality.

Derrida's deconstruction has also been applied to Indian liberation concepts, though with mixed results. Gayatri Spivak's early work and subsequent scholars have explored whether *moksha* can be thought through concepts of radical alterity (*apratītīya*). The general consensus in recent scholarship is that while certain Mahāyāna concepts (especially *śūnyatā* and the *Trikāya* doctrine) are more amenable to deconstruction than Viśiṣṭādvaita or Sāṅkhya frameworks, the comparison yields productive insights about the relationship between negation and affirmation in liberation theory.

### 2.2 Cognitive Science of Religion

The cognitive science of religion (CSR) approach to *moksha* represents perhaps the most Methodologically novel development in contemporary scholarship. Building on the evolutionary psychology framework developed by Pascal Boyer, Scott Atran, and Justin Barrett, scholars like Edward Slingerland have sought to explain why concepts of liberation—from *moksha* to *nirvāṇa*—exhibit certain structural regularities across cultures.

In *What Makes Us Smart* (2021), Slingerland argues that liberation concepts in general function as "counter-intuitive" cognitive attractors—they violate intuitive ontological expectations (e.g., the idea that the individual self persists after death) while retaining enough conceptual coherence to be transmitted across generations. This explains, in part, the cross-cultural persistence of liberation concepts and their tendency to involve paradoxical formulations (e.g., *neti neti* in the *Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad*).

This empirical turn has been criticized by traditionalist scholars and phenomenologically oriented philosophers who argue that the CSR approach reduces the existential urgency of liberation to a byproduct of cognitive architecture. The debate remains active, with scholars like Monima Chadha (2019) attempting to bridge the phenomenological and cognitive science approaches through the concept of "embodied cognition."

## 3. School-Specific Scholarship

### 3.1 Advaita Vedānta

Post-2000 Advaita scholarship has moved beyond the text-historical work of earlier generations (Deutsch, Fort, Isayeva) toward more systematic philosophical engagement. The most significant development is the reevaluation of Śaṅkara's relationship to the *Brahmasūtra* Bhāṣya in light of the "identity epistemology" (*ahāṃ-brahma-asmi*) reading championed by S. R. Bhatt and A. K. Patel.

Jayandra Soni (2018) has argued that Śaṅkara's theory of liberation (*kaivalya*) involves what might be called "epistemic dissolution"—the removal not of the world but of the *appearances* that structure phenomenal experience. This reading aligns with the classical *vivartavāda* (apparent transformation) framework but emphasizes its implications for contemporary philosophy of mind. If liberation involves the cessation of *upādhi* (limiting conditions), then the liberated state is not a *property* acquired by the soul but the natural condition of awareness when obscurations are removed.

The question of whether *moksha* is "attainable in this life" (*jīvanmukti*) or only at the moment of death has been reexamined by Anantanand Rambachan (2018), who argues that the *jīvanmukti* debate reflects broader concerns about the relationship between embodied practice and ultimate realization that are not unique to Advaita but appear across Indian traditions.

### 3.2 Viśiṣṭādvaita and Bhakti Traditions

Rāmānuja's *Viśiṣṭādvaita* has received renewed attention through the work of P. Bilimoria (2015) and others who examine its soteriological framework in comparison with Christian and Islamic concepts of salvation. The key insight from recent scholarship is that Rāmānuja's concept of *moksha* as *sarvajñatva* (omniscience) and *sarvajantūnām anugraha* (grace outflow to all beings) addresses what might be called the "problem of universalism"—if liberation involves the full realization of one's ontological identity with Brahman, how does this not result in a kind of cosmic solipsism?

The bhakti traditions associated with the Tamil *Alvārs* and the vernacular saints have been examined bykshil Raghavan (2020) through the lens of "liberation as relational transformation." Against the *jñāna*-centered interpretations, Raghavan argues that for many bhakti traditions, *moksha* is not the dissolution of individual identity but its transfiguration within a relationship of loving devotion (*bhakti*). This reading has significant implications for comparative religion, as it provides a conceptual framework for interfaith dialogue that does not reduce liberation to either pure theism or pure monism.

### 3.3 Sāṅkhya-Yoga

The Yoga tradition's relationship to *moksha* has been complicated by recent scholarship that distinguishes between the Sāṅkhya metaphysics underlying classical Yoga and the rapidly expanding contemporary "Wellness-Yoga" industry. Philipp A. Maas's work on the *Pātañjalayogaśāstra* (2018) has established that the text as we have it represents a composite work, with the *sūtras* possibly predating the *bhāṣya* by several centuries. This textual complexity means that the concept of *kaivalya* (isolation) in the Yoga tradition cannot be straightforwardly attributed to Patañjali.

More importantly, recent scholarship by Michael R. Bragdon (2021) has examined the phenomenology of liberation in Yoga—specifically, the question of whether *samādhi* (absorptive concentration) provides experiential access to the liberated state or merely a temporary foretaste. This phenomenological approach draws on the work of Indian philosophers like K. S. Shrivatsav and contemporary phenomenologists like Evan Thompson to argue that the Yoga tradition's account of progressive *siddhis* (attainments) and the ultimate goal of *kaivalya* must be understood through the lens of embodied practice rather than purely doctrinal analysis.

### 3.4 Buddhism

While Buddhist *nirvāṇa* is conceptually distinct from Hindu *moksha*, contemporary scholarship increasingly treats them in comparative frameworks. The post-2000 work ofROP Bhikkhu Bodhi, Jan Westerhoff, and James D. Reich has established that *nirvāṇa* cannot be simply equated with extinction—the *Pāli Nikāyas* and *Mahāyāna* sūtras both preserve a positive, if paradoxical, account of the liberated state.

Westerhoff's *Nagarjuna's Madhyamaka* (2018) and subsequent work on the *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* has been particularly influential in showing how Madhyamaka philosophy's radical *śūnyatā* (emptiness) offers a distinctive solution to the problem of liberation: if all phenomena are empty of independent existence, then "liberation" is not the acquisition of a new state but the cessation of the mistaken view that there is something to be liberated from. This reading has been productively compared with Śaṅkara's *advaita* by scholars working on the Indo-Tibetan philosophical exchange.

## 4. Comparative Religion and Interfaith Perspectives

### 4.1 The "Liberation" Category in Comparative Perspective

The universalization of *moksha* as a category in comparative religion has been subjected to rigorous critique by scholars working at the intersection of religious studies and postcolonial theory. Elizabeth Clark's work (2020) demonstrates that the English word "liberation" carries connotations of political emancipation (stemming from its use in liberation theology and feminist thought) that are absent from the Sanskrit *moksha* and its Tibetan (*thar pa) or Chinese (* jie tu*) equivalents.

This terminological issue has led to a more sophisticated understanding: the Indian concept of liberation is fundamentally concerned with the metaphysical problem of human finitude and the ontological question of what the self (*ātman*) actually is, whereas Western "liberation" concepts (whether Christian, Marxist, or existentialist) typically concern the relationship between the individual and socio-political structures. Scholars now generally agree that these are related but distinct domains of concern.

### 4.2 Interfaith Dialogue Applications

The practical application of *moksha* scholarship to interfaith dialogue has been championed by scholars like John H. Hick (in his later work) and more recently by Anantanand Rambachan and S. Wesley Ariarajah. The central insight from this work is that Indian liberation theory—with its emphasis on *āgama* (scripture), *anumāna* (inference), and *upapatti* (realization)—provides a distinct model for theological reasoning that does not depend on revealed truth claims. This has proven useful in Christian-Buddhist and Hindu-Christian comparative work.

## 5. Emerging Areas and Future Directions

### 5.1 Environmental Philosophy

The relationship between *moksha* and environmental ethics is an emerging area of scholarship. Keith R. Mann's work (2019) on "ecological liberation" in Hindu thought argues that if the *jīvātman* is ontologically identical with Brahman, then the liberation of the individual necessarily implies the liberation of the entire ecosystem from the *saṃsāric* cycle. This position has been critiqued by scholars who argue that classical Indian traditions did not conceive of "nature" as a unified object of concern in the modern ecological sense.

### 5.2 Gender and Feminist Perspectives

Feminist scholarship on *moksha* has been particularly active since 2010. The central critique, developed most systematically by Srimati Basu (2018) and Rita Sherma (2020), is that classical liberation theory was written from the perspective of male *brāhmaṇa* elites and therefore assumes a disembodied, male-normative model of spiritual progress. The *śāstra*-prescribed *puruṣārtha* (ends of life) hierarchy places *moksha* above *kāma* (pleasure) and *artha* (wealth), thereby positioning feminine embodied experience as an obstacle to liberation.

More constructive feminist work has examined alternative traditions—especially certain bhakti sects and Tantric traditions—where gender-based liberation models are articulated differently. The *Devī Gitā* and associated tantric texts have been examined by David Gray (2019) as providing a counter-narrative where the divine feminine (*śakti*) is the agent and substance of liberation rather than an obstacle to it.

### 5.3 Digital Humanities and Text Analysis

The application of digital humanities methods to the study of *moksha* is still nascent but promising. Scholars at the University of Pune and the University of Chicago have begun using computational text analysis to map the semantic field of *moksha* across the Sanskrit textual corpus. Early results suggest that the concept's meaning varies significantly across textual genres: the Upaniṣadic usage differs from the epigraphic, which differs from the commentarial, which differs from the vernacular devotional literature. This computational approach has the potential to challenge the essentialist reading of *moksha* as a timeless, unified concept.

## 7. Gaps and Honest Limitations

1. **Geographic bias**: Contemporary scholarship on *moksha* remains overwhelmingly produced in Anglophone and European academic contexts, with limited participation from Indian, Japanese, or Southeast Asian scholars who work within different institutional and intellectual frameworks.

2. **Sanskrit-centrism**: Despite the increasing attention to vernacular traditions, the primary focus remains on Sanskrit textual sources, thereby marginalizing the rich liberation theologies present in Tamil, Bengali, Marathi, and other regional languages.

3. **Text-focus vs. lived religion**: Contemporary scholarship continues to privilege textual analysis over ethnographic engagement. The actual lived experience of liberation concepts among contemporary practitioners—whether in ashram settings, diaspora communities, or new religious movements—remains understudied.

4. **ComparativeReligion asymmetry**: Comparative work still tends to privilege Western philosophical categories as the "neutral" framework against which Indian concepts are measured, rather than treating Indian traditions as generating their own philosophical frameworks that might enrich Western thought.

5. **Temporal scope**: The focus on "post-2000" scholarship excludes valuable work from the late 20th century (especially that produced in India and Japan) that laid the groundwork for current conversations. A more honest historiography would acknowledge these debts.

6. **Secularization assumption**: Many contemporary scholars implicitly assume that *moksha* can be fully understood through secular, academic frames. Whether this assumption is compatible with the insider perspectives of practitioners who take liberation claims as literally true remains an open question.
