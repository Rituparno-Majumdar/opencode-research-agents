# Karma in Western Philosophy

## Executive Summary

The concept of moral causation in Western thought differs fundamentally from the Indic कर्म (karma). While Indic traditions treat karma as cosmological law operating across lifetimes, Western philosophy tends to locate moral causation within:
1. Virtue ethics (Aristotle's hexis)
2. Stoic determinism and acceptance
3. Kantian deontology (moral law within reason)
4. Schopenhauer's pessimistic metaphysics

This research identifies key parallels and divergences between Indic कर्म (karma) and Western frameworks.

---

## 1. Greek Origins — Moira and Tyche

### 1.1 Homeric Concept

The Greek concept closest to कर्म (karma) is μοῖρα (moira) — fate:

> μοῖραν δ' οὐ τινά φημι πεφυγμένον ἔμμεναι ἀνδρῶν
> (moiran d' ou tina phemi pephygmenon emmenai andrōn)
> — "No man escapes his fate"

The distinction: Greek μοῖρα (moira) is externally decreed (by gods, by cosmos); Indic कर्म (karma) is internally generated (by one's own actions).

### 1.2 Aristotle — Hexis and Moral Habit

> ἔξις δ' ἐστὶν ὁ τρόπος τῆς κινήσεως
> (exis d' estin ho tropos tēs kinēseōs)
> — "Hexis is the disposition or habit"

Aristotle's ἕξις (hexis) — moral habit — represents a within-lifetime moral causation: repeated actions form character, character determines future actions.

The causal chain:
> πρᾶξις → ἕξις → δόξα → πρᾶξις
> (praxis → hexis → doxa → praxis)
> — action → habit → opinion → action

## 2. Stoic Philosophy — Determinism and Acceptance

### 2.1 The Logos and Providence

The Stoic λόγος (logos) — rational principle — governs all events:

> τῷ λόγῳ συνεχόμενον
> (tō logō synechomenon)
> — "Held together by the Logos"

This is closer to कर्म (karma) than Greek μοῖρα (moira): the Logos is an immanent causal principle, not external decree.

### 2.2 Epictetus — The Inner Realm

> ἐν τοῖς οἰκείοις περισπευδέτω
> (en tois oikeiois perispeudetō)
> — "Let him busy himself with his own affairs"

For Epictetus, what we control (prohairesis — will/choice) is the only true agent. External events are fated. This maps partially onto कर्म (karma):

| Stoic | Indic | Difference |
|-------|-------|------------|
| Prohairesis (choice) | Intent in karma | Both internal |
| External fate | Karma's fruits | Stoic fate external; karma is consequence |

---

## 3. Kant — Practical Reason and Moral Law

Kant's categorical imperative:

> Handle so, dass du die Menschheit... jederzeit zugleich als Zweck, niemals bloss als Mittel brauchst
> — "Act so that you treat humanity... always as an end, never merely as a means"

The moral law is categorical — it applies regardless of consequences. This contrasts with कर्म (karma), where consequences (phala) are central.

The divergence:
- Kant: duty is rational, consequence irrelevant
- कर्म (karma): action is measured by its fruit

---

## 4. Schopenhauer — The World as Will

Schopenhauer's closest Indic parallel:

> Der Wille zum Leben ist der Kern des Leidens
> — "The will-to-live is the core of suffering"

> जन्म मृत्युः पुनर्जन्म
> (janma mṛtyuḥ punarjanma)
> — "Birth, death, rebirth" — the Indic cycle

Schopenhauer's पुनर्जन्म (punarjanma) — rebirth — is driven by the Will. The path to salvation (Erlösung) is the denial of the Will:

> Aufhebung des Willens zum Leben
> — "The cancelling of the will-to-live"

---

## 5. Process Philosophy — Whitehead

Whitehead's prehension:

> Each occasion prehends its past
> — "Each moment carries forward its history"

The process view offers a third alternative between strict karma (mechanical causation) and pure chance.

## Gaps & Honest Limitations

1. **Medieval Christian theology**: Augustine on grace and predestination underexplored
2. **Existentialism**: Sartre's radical freedom contradicts karma's causal structure
3. **American pragmatism**: Dewey's moral causation without metaphysics
