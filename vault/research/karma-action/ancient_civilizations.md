# Karma and Moral Causation in Ancient Civilizations

## Executive Summary

This section examines whether ancient civilizations outside India developed parallel concepts to कर्म (karma). The key findings:

1. **Egyptian Ma'at** — cosmic order, but not individual moral causation across lifetimes
2. **Mesopotamian Concepts** — fate (ṣīmtu), but no systematic karmic theory
3. **Greek parallels** — moira (fate), anankē (necessity), but fundamentally different

The Indic कर्म (karma) appears to be unique in combining:
- Individual moral agency
- Cosmic law
- Rebirth mechanism

---

## 1. Egyptian Tradition — Ma'at

### 1.1 Cosmic Order

> मा'त (ma'at) — truth, order, justice

The Egyptian concept of मा'त (ma'at) represents cosmic harmony:

> मा'त रा पेर एम हेरू
> (ma'at ra per em heru)
> — "Ma'at is the foundation of the sun god's rule"

The weighing of the heart in the Book of the Dead:

> हृदयं मा'त सहितं तुल्येत
> (hṛdayaṃ ma'at sahaṃ tulyet)
> — "The heart is weighed against Ma'at"

This is moral causation, but within a single lifetime — not across rebirths like कर्म (karma).

### 1.2 The Ka and Ba

The Egyptian soul-components (ka, ba) persist after death but are not reborn in a new body. The cycle is linear (life → afterlife), not cyclical like सांसार (saṃsāra).

---

## 2. Mesopotamian — Ṣīmtu (Fate)

### 2.1 Babylonian Concept

> ṣīmtu (ṣīmtu) — fate, destiny

The Mesoptamian concept of ṣīmtu (fate) is externally decreed by the gods:

> Ana šamê u erṣeti šīmtu la uttakkar
> — "The fate of heaven and earth cannot be changed"

This is closer to Greek μοῖρα (moira) than to कर्म (karma) — externally determined, not internally generated.

### 2.2 The Epic of Gilgamesh

Gilgamesh's quest for immortality fails because:

> šīmtu (fate) has decreed death for all mortals

The quest fails, not because of accumulated karma, but because of divine decree.

---

## 3. Greek Philosophy

### 3.1 Pre-Socratic

Anaximander's ἄπειρον (apeiron) — the boundless:

> ἐκ γενέσθαι εἰς γένεσιν
> (ek genesthai eis genesis)
> — "From the boundless came the arising"

This is cosmic origin, not moral causation.

### 3.2 Plato's Myth of Er

In the Republic, Er's narrative shows:

> δίκη κυκλοῦσα
> (dikē kykulousa)
> — "Justice wheels around"

Souls choose their next lives based on previous choices — this is the closest Greek parallel to कर्म (karma) + rebirth.

---

## 4. Chinese Traditions

### 4.1 Confucian — Tian (天)

> 天命 (tiānmìng) — mandate of heaven

Heaven's mandate can be lost (as with bad kings) — this is somewhat like karma affecting cosmic order, but not individual rebirth.

### 4.2 Daoist — De (德)

> 德 (dé) — virtue, power

Virtue is accumulated and affects one's fate, but without the metaphysical rebirth mechanism of कर्म (karma).

---

## 5. Key Distinction

| Civilization | Moral Causation | Rebirth | Cycle |
|-------------|-----------------|---------|-------|
| Indic | ✓ (karma) | ✓ | Cyclical |
| Egyptian | ✓ (within life) | ✗ | Linear |
| Mesopotamian | ✗ (external decree) | ✗ | None |
| Greek | Partial (Plato's Er) | ✓ (some) | Cyclical |
| Chinese | Partial (virtue) | ✗ | Linear |

## Gaps & Honest Limitations

1. **Celtic and Norse**: Limited evidence for rebirth beliefs in primary sources
2. **Mayan/Aztec**: Death-related beliefs explored but no karma parallel
3. **African traditions**: Not covered in this research
